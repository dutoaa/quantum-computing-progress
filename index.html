Beauty and charm jet identification with end-to-end training on a superconducting quantum processor

Lucio Anderlini, Pietro Asinari, Gianluca Bertaina, Davide Calonico, Marco Coïsson, Alessio Gianelle

The identification of heavy-flavor jets, i.e. collimated streams of particles originating from the fragmentation of beauty and charm quarks produced in proton-proton collisions, represents one of the most challenging classification tasks in measurements at Large Hadron Collider experiments. In recent years, advanced machine learning techniques have been successfully employed to address this problem, while quantum machine learning has emerged as a promising alternative approach. However, the high dimensionality of the problem, typically involving hundreds of observables, raises an open question: whether the current generation of quantum computers, characterized by limited qubit count, can effectively tackle such complex tasks. In this work, a variational quantum circuit for the classification of b- and c-jets at the LHCb experiment is implemented on a 5-qubit superconducting quantum computer manufactured by IQM and installed at the Politecnico di Torino. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of a quantum algorithm for jet identification using simulated data from an LHC experiment in which all stages of the machine-learning workflow, including training and testing, are performed on real quantum hardware. The results demonstrate that the impact of hardware-induced quantum noise does not significantly degrade the performance. A framework for the evaluation of the training time on the quantum hardware and for the circuit optimization is also proposed. These findings pave the way for future applications on larger quantum devices based on superconducting qubits, featuring a larger number of qubits enabling the encoding of information beyond the reach of classical simulation and providing a step toward investigating whether quantum advantage may eventually be achievable in high-energy physics classification tasks.

Programming Quantum Circuit Parameters with Large Language Models

Tiago de Souza Farias, Caio Moraes Porto, Matheus da Silva Fonseca, Nicolas Armando Cabrera Carpi, Anderson de Araujo Oliveira, Celso Jorge Villas-Boas

Large language models (LLMs) have recently demonstrated the ability to generate parameters for variational quantum circuits without conventional optimization. However, the conditions under which this approach is effective remain unclear. Here, we investigate a frozen, general-purpose LLM that generates complete circuit parameter vectors from symbolic circuit and Hamiltonian descriptions, with optional iterative energy feedback. We evaluate the approach on general random Hamiltonians, structured parent Hamiltonians, and molecular systems. For general Hamiltonians, LLM-generated parameters exhibit larger energy errors than conventional Adam optimization, while additional feedback generally provides limited improvement. In contrast, for structured Hamiltonians, the LLM can generate promising low-energy parameter configurations, suggesting that identifiable physical and mathematical structure facilitates parameter inference. We also show that direct LLM predictions of minimum energies can violate the Hamiltonian spectral lower bound, whereas generated circuit parameters allow independent evaluation of physically realizable energy expectation values. Finally, we investigate blind circuit interpretation and find that the LLM can predict measurement outcomes even when it fails to identify the underlying quantum algorithm. These findings highlight the role of problem structure, the limitations of iterative LLM optimization, and the importance of independently verifiable predictions in quantum parameter programming.

Multi-head Quantum Neural Networks: Replacing Transformer Blocks with NISQ Modules inside a 30-Billion-Parameter LLM

Maforikan Amoussou, Borja Aizpurua, Sukhbinder Singh, Román Orús, Hans-Arno Jacobsen

We introduce the multi-head quantum neural network (QNN), a quantum module compatible with noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices that can replace transformer blocks inside pre-trained large language models (LLMs) with little loss in accuracy and with far fewer parameters than the blocks it replaces. Encoding a model's $D$ hidden neurons into a qubit register is the obstacle: amplitude encoding scales poorly in circuit depth and angle encoding in qubit count, so neither is practical at production-scale width. We instead partition the hidden space into $k$ disjoint heads, angle-encode each into an $n_q$-qubit register, process the heads in parallel, and couple them through constrained classical linear layers, the mixers. Performing surgery on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507, we show that a single multi-head QNN with $145\,281$ trainable parameters ($D=2048$, $k=32$, $n_q=8$, $L=4$) can replace a contiguous span of eight transformer blocks, displacing $16.33\%$ of the model's parameters. Ablations show that, for a single replaced block, the quantum heads (circuits followed by a learned readout), and not the classical mixers alone, recover the displaced function, whereas for fused spans the mixers alone come within $1$-$3\%$ of the full module in perplexity. Whether this benefit is specific to trained, entangling quantum circuits is not established: controls that switch the entangler off (two seeds), or freeze the circuit at its initial or at uniformly random angles (one seed), beat the full module on held-out perplexity, and a parameter-matched classical multilayer perceptron remains a few percent ahead of the full module. We train classically with exact statevector simulation, then execute the quantum heads of our fusion checkpoints on a $156$-qubit IBM Heron processor, showing that NISQ-scale quantum modules can be installed in a production-scale LLM and run on hardware.

A Unified Benchmarking Study of Variational Quantum Eigensolver Ansatz for Electronic Structure Problems

Julie Maria Raju, Abhoy Kole, Julen Larrucea, Kamalika Datta, Lennart Weingarten, Rolf Drechsler

Electronic structure calculations become computationally expensive on classical computers as electron correlation increases. Quantum computing offers a promising alternative through hybrid quantum-classical Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) on Noisy-Intermediate Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices. The choice of ansatz plays a pivotal role in the performance and scalability of the VQE, yet selecting an optimal ansatz remains challenging. Existing approaches including chemically inspired ansatz, adaptive schemes and Hardware Efficient Ansätze (HEA) lack a unified comparison. This gap makes the scalability and trade-offs of each method unclear for varying molecular sizes. This work presents a systematic benchmarking study comparing Unitary Couples Cluster Singles and Doubles Ansatz (UCCSD), fermionic ADAPT-VQE, BasicPCA HEA, and Excitation Preserving HEA across molecules of varying size like $H_2$, $LiH$, $N_2$. We evaluate trade-offs among circuit depth, parameter count, ground-state energy error, and NISQ feasibility. ADAPT-VQE achieves accuracy comparable to UCCSD with lower resource overhead, but becomes impractical for modest system sizes ($\sim$10 electrons) due to increasing circuit complexity. In contrast, HEA reduces hardware costs but requires more parameters and sacrifices accuracy, with performance strongly dependent on molecular size. The Excitation Preserving ansatz remains below chemical accuracy for $H_2$ and $LiH$, and fails for $N_2$, underscoring the need to balance expressibility and trainability. Overall, these results highlight key trade-offs between accuracy and resource efficiency across VQE ansätze, emphasizing the need to improve ADAPT-VQE scalability and HEA accuracy while addressing optimization challenges for near-term quantum hardware.

Hierarchical Decoding for Quantum Error Correction

Xulong Tang, Narges Alavisamani, Joan Camps, Matthew Cha, Elise Jennings, Jack O'Connor

Fault-tolerant quantum computing requires classical decoders that can keep up with syndrome data streams and supply correction information and logical measurement outcomes to the quantum control system. Meeting these requirements at scale requires high throughput while controlling the end-to-end latency from readout to usable correction. In this paper, we organize decoding algorithms, execution strategies, and code constructions using common classifications and examine their performance and trade-offs under the reported evaluation conditions. We distinguish decoder-level hierarchy, where multiple decoding stages or resources cooperate to decode a single code, from code-level hierarchy, where the code construction itself introduces multiple decoding levels. We first review single-code hierarchical decoders and their cascaded predecoding and windowed, streaming, and distributed implementations. We then study hierarchical and concatenated codes, focusing on how decoding is coordinated across code levels through hard decisions, soft reliability information, or syndrome information. We also examine how qubit modalities shape decoding workloads and timing requirements, and review how different decoding platforms meet these requirements, including central processing units (CPUs), graphics processing units (GPUs), field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs), application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs), and cryogenic platforms. By assessing design trade-offs, this paper clarifies connections across the literature and provides a shared foundation for QEC design exploration. It also identifies gaps in performance reporting and system integration to guide future research toward practical quantum error correction.

Infinite or indefinite: the query cost of simulating the quantum switch

Dantong Li

The quantum switch applies two operations in a coherent superposition of their causal orders and is a simple example of indefinite causal order. No quantum circuit that uses each operation once in a definite order can even describe it, yet photonic experiments have demonstrated its physical realization. We ask how many calls to the black-box operations a quantum computer in the circuit model needs to reproduce the switch. If one of the black boxes is known to be unitary, three calls suffice. For general channels on a $d$-dimensional system, we prove three results. In all three, circuits may use classical feed-forward and unlimited ancillary quantum memory. First, no finite number of calls suffices for exact simulation in the worst case. Second, approximation to any fixed error below $1/2$, measured in half the diamond norm, requires $Ω(d^{1/4})$ calls, exponential in the number of qubits. Third, an ordinary circuit can require exponentially many calls to obtain a single bit that the switch extracts with a few uses. We consider a device whose actual noise is dephasing in a fixed unknown basis and test it against a theoretical depolarizing model. The two channels have the same entanglement and average gate fidelities, so these figures alone do not distinguish them. At fixed noise strength, the quantum switch tells them apart with a number of device uses independent of $d$, whereas the circuit model requires $Ω(d^{1/16})$ calls. Noise characterization is needed for quantum error correction, and this gives a provable query advantage of the switch in a task relevant to practical quantum computing. We invite future work that extends such advantages to more practical computing tasks.

Hybrid Classical-Quantum Optimization for CVRPTW: A Systems Framework with Provable Feasibility and Empirical Hardware Limits

Syamasundar Gopasana, Swatantra Singh, Rohan Joydhar, Shubhashis Sengupta

Solving large-scale Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problems with Time Windows (CVRPTW) remains challenging because strict operational constraints and scale limit both exact and heuristic methods. We present a hybrid classical-quantum architecture combining Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) with vehicle-level Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO), solved using Toshiba SQBM+ and QAOA on Rigetti Ankaa-3 and IQM Emerald quantum processors. The decomposition avoids the variable growth and constraint-enforcement difficulties of monolithic CVRPTW QUBO formulations. We evaluate the framework on five realistic benchmarks derived from London traffic and location data, spanning 10--70 customers, using a validation pipeline enforcing feasibility and monotone improvement over classical baselines. SQBM+ achieves verified fleet-distance reductions of 2.80% (7,927 m) for the 25-customer instance and 0.22% (1,186 m) for the 55-customer instance. QAOA experiments establish a current hardware boundary for reliable constraint satisfaction at subproblems of up to 4 customers (16 qubits). Diagnostic results identify assignment-level time-window tightness, rather than local route optimization, as the dominant scalability bottleneck. The results demonstrate a practical near-term role for hybrid classical-quantum optimization while establishing empirical limits for current quantum hardware.

QSim-Arena: an evolving developer for quantum simulation advantage

Jue Xu, Yue Wang, Qi Zhao

Where practical advantage emerges in quantum dynamics simulation remains a central debate in quantum computing. Claimed advantages can be rapidly narrowed or overturned by stronger classical methods, while progress on both sides can outpace the journal publication cycle. We present QSim-Arena, a research framework in which AI agents combine a closed loop of verification and benchmarking with open exploration. Hamiltonians, initial states and accuracy targets for evolved states or observable expectation values provide explicit specifications for evaluating simulation methods. Agents translate published techniques into executable components, reproduce reported results and benchmark their performance across diverse Hamiltonian families. Competing classical methods are implemented and independently crosschecked, providing credible baselines for end-to-end quantum resource estimates based on rigorous error bounds and empirical calibration. Alongside this closed loop, agents recombine classical simulation techniques and co-design fault-tolerant quantum circuits using knowledge extracted from the literature. Initial applications improve baseline resource estimates through the integration of established techniques. QSim-Arena thus treats quantum advantage assessment as an evolving, reproducible research process that can respond to advances in algorithms, error analysis and hardware.

Training quantum neural networks one job at a time

Muhammad Faryad, Rabab Alomairy

When a quantum processor is reached through a cloud queue, a variational algorithm pays for every round trip, and a single round trip can carry many circuits. The classical optimizer therefore decides how much is learned per job, not only per shot. We study this for a quantum neural network classifier. We compare optimizers that are given the same number of jobs and the same shots per job, and that differ only in how they fill each job. This comparison treats hybrid quantum-classical training as an execution-efficiency problem: how to obtain useful optimization progress from each costly interaction with a quantum processor. The resulting perspective connects optimizer design to overhead amortization and efficient use of accelerator resources. A direct port of a batched pattern search that worked for the quantum approximate optimization algorithm stalls once the model has a few dozen parameters. What does transfer is the principle behind it: fill each job with a batch of parameter vectors that is fixed before the job is sent. For circuits built from Pauli rotations the natural batch is the parameter-shift stencil. We show that its gradient error falls as the inverse of the shots per job, while the error of simultaneous-perturbation methods (SPSA) has a floor that no number of shots removes. In simulation, batched parameter-shift training needs between a fifth and a half of the jobs SPSA needs, for 24 to 48 parameters, and the ordering holds at two noise levels. A new structure-aware stencil search reads the same kind of rows as sinusoids and also beats SPSA. On IBM's ibm_fez processor, in three interleaved runs at six and eight qubits, the batched optimizer reached SPSA's final training loss within 4 to 20 of its 50 jobs. Its test accuracy was clearly higher at eight qubits; at six qubits the difference was not significant. All hardware numbers can be re-derived from the raw shots.

Quantum Material Generation via Coherent Hamiltonian Search

Jiang Yuhan, Matthew Otten

Discovering new materials with targeted properties is a central goal of materials science, yet the space of candidates is vast. Data-driven generative models learn the distribution of known materials, but they extrapolate poorly, and a generated structure is not known to be stable. A material's properties follow from its Hamiltonian, and a quantum computer can calculate them from it. We introduce the quantum material generative algorithm (QMage), a coherent search, guided by the Hamiltonian, over crystalline configurations of atomic composition, unit cell structure, and atomic positions, for one whose calculated properties meet the targets to within a given error tolerance. The computation is a single coherent quantum circuit in which each configuration's Hamiltonian is block-encoded, its target eigenstate isolated and properties evaluated, and the satisfying configurations amplified by fixed-point search and measured. One framework covers many targets, from formation energy and band gaps to elasticity and conductivity. We design the algorithm for the mature fault-tolerant era and estimate its cost in logical qubits and Toffoli depth from a classically routine benchmark to targets where established methods are unreliable. The approach generates candidate materials from first principles, rather than generalizing from those known, and could enable materials discovery when the target properties are difficult to predict classically.

From optimization to construction: Expanding quantum measurement designs

Taotao Zhao, Qianjun Yao, Mengdi Sun, He Li

Estimating molecular energies on quantum computers requires repeated measurements of many Pauli terms, making sampling a major resource cost. Measurement design chooses which compatible terms are measured together and allocates a finite shot budget among bases to minimize the variance of an energy estimator. We introduce variance-guided measurement group construction (VGMC), which reduces variance beyond optimization on existing measurement bases by changing which Hamiltonian terms share measurement shots. After including every Hamiltonian term measurable in each existing basis, we jointly optimize estimator coefficients and shot allocations to separate inefficient use of these measurements from limitations of their measurement combinations. We compare a candidate's benefit with the cost of withdrawing shots from existing bases, and use the resulting scores to guide greedy construction of qubit-wise commuting groups followed by joint re-optimization. With exact covariances, VGMC outperformed the state-of-the-art measurement design methods across the molecular benchmarks where it introduced additional measurement bases, achieving variance reductions of 18.19%-23.38%. The gains persisted with approximate covariances, and simulations with finite shots indicated lower estimated shot requirements near chemical accuracy. These results show that optimizing how measurements are combined can also guide which terms are measured together, offering a route to reducing the sampling overhead of variational quantum simulation while retaining local measurements on current and near-term devices.

Phase Interference and Transverse Catalysts Govern the Noise Fragility of Long-Range Variational Quantum Circuits

Longhui Shen, Fangzhou Jin, Xiaoxia Yi, Bin Guo

Long-range entangling gates shorten variational state preparation, but they also accelerate operator spreading across the register, linking nonlocality to a presumed noise fragility. Here we show that the single-fault fragility $F$ of a variational circuit is governed by how fault phases interfere on the target Hamiltonian, not by how far faults spread. For Heisenberg chains we prove that two faults with identical extensive support can incur either bounded or extensive energy damage, in the exact ratio $N/2$. A phase-matched longitudinal halo remains energetically dark until ignited by transverse catalysts that break a symmetry of the remote register, namely spatially nonuniform single-qubit rotations or the pair-flip component of two-qubit $XX/YY$ gates, whereas uniform rotations and magnetization-conserving exchange gates leave it strictly dark. In noisy training with full-width long-range $ZZ$ gates, suppressing either catalyst alone leaves circuits trapped on an ignited plateau sustained by remote $XY$ couplings, whereas suppressing both eliminates the plateau, achieves minimal fragility despite larger fault operators, and halves the dephasing error. Training is driven by a race between catalyst pruning and gradient force balance, as annealing the transverse catalysts out of trapped circuits rescues every trajectory while annealing the entire longitudinal sector does not. Combined with the gate-count exposure $λ=pK$, a single fragility measurement accurately predicts the noise-induced energy shift across circuit architectures and noise strengths, reducing ansatz design to a trade-off between coherent expressivity and gate count.

Ensemble phase transitions in measurement-unitary hybrid quantum circuits

Saptarshi Mandal, Sthitadhi Roy

The universal properties of state ensembles generated by quantum dynamics provide a refined lens into Hilbert-space ergodicity and deep thermalisation, or lack thereof. We investigate deep-ergodicity breaking transitions of an ancillary qubit entangled with a system undergoing dynamics via hybrid quantum circuits, where unitary evolution is interspersed with projective measurements. Specifically, we analyse the statistical properties of two distinct state ensembles: the trajectory ensemble (TE) conditioned on the measurement outcomes of the hybrid circuit, and the projected ensemble (PE) generated by further projective measurements of the system at the final temporal boundary. We find that both ensembles undergo non-analytic changes -- ensemble phase transitions -- at the critical point of the measurement-induced purification transition, despite their first moments remaining completely maximally mixed and blind to the transition. For Haar-random circuits, the TE transitions from a trivial identity ensemble to the uniform Haar ensemble, while the PE transitions from the Haar ensemble to a trivial singleton pure state. In Clifford circuits, absence of non-stabiliserness limits the PE to a $1$-design, while the TE forms a $k$-design for $k \leq 3$. We demonstrate that these genuine ensemble transitions offer a clear physical mechanism for the measurement-induced entanglement or purification transitions, illustrating how the capacity to ergodically steer a quantum subsystem shifts from the temporal boundary measurements in the mixed phase to the bulk trajectory measurements in the pure phase.
