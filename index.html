Soft Mixture of Quantum Experts for Hybrid Quantum-Classical Learning

Valeria Repetto, Romina D'Aurizio, Piero Farinelli

Quantum Machine Learning (QML) has attracted increasing interest as a possible route to construct feature representations that are difficult to reproduce with classical models. However, near-term QML architectures based on trainable parametrized quantum circuits remain affected by limited hardware resources, noise, and severe optimization difficulties such as barren plateaus. In this work we propose Soft-MoQE, a Soft Mixture of Quantum Experts architecture that replaces variational quantum training with the input-dependent selection and combination of fixed quantum feature maps. The model is composed of a heterogeneous pool of non-trainable quantum circuits, each defining a distinct embedding, and a classical gating network that learns how to weight these embeddings for each data point. We benchmark Soft-MoQE on three PCA-reduced tasks: MNIST parity, MNIST 10-class, and FashionMNIST. Soft-MoQE outperforms the single best expert, the uniform average of all experts and a linear baseline, achieving task-dependent performance comparable to that of a trainable quantum neural network, without optimizing any quantum parameter and with a quantum training cost that is lower by orders of magnitude. The analysis of the class- conditioned expert usage shows that the learned routing combines a few experts for each input, with a selection that depends on the class. These results support the learned routing among fixed quantum circuits as a trainability-aware design principle for hybrid QML.

Variational Relevance-Informed Sparsification of Term Measurements for Variational Quantum Algorithms

Yifeng Peng, Bin Yan, Harshal Shah, Srinivasan Raghuraman, Kim Wagner, Sunpreet Singh Arora

Repeated measurements are a major bottleneck in variational quantum algorithms (VQAs). We uncover and characterize parameter-specific sparsity in the matrix of qubit-wise commuting (QWC) group contributions to parameter-shift gradients: most absolute contribution mass concentrates in a subset of groups whose relevance varies across parameters and optimization states. Building on this structure, we introduce a learned controller that predicts group relevance from Hamiltonian, circuit, light-cone, and current-state features, then selects measurements through a tunable coverage threshold. Exact contributions provide offline supervision without being required at deployment. Across 15 molecular VQE settings, our method saves 49.6% of nominal gradient-measurement shots with a mean absolute energy gap of 5.67 mHa to Full-QWC. We then ask whether large language models (LLMs) can solve the same selection problem without fine-tuning. The results reveal both promise and a clear limitation: LLMs can identify useful measurement groups, yet without guidance, local models do not consistently prioritize the physical information that matters most. Our learned controller uses these cues more effectively. This contrast points toward a broader direction for AI-assisted science: combining the flexibility of general-purpose models with explicit physical representations, task-specific objectives, and domain-grounded validation.

Nonlinear function regression using quantum ensemble learning

Yueen Chen, Mohammad Sharifian, Abolfazl Bayat

Recent advances in artificial intelligence and quantum technologies have driven the emergence of quantum machine learning, yet imperfections in near-term quantum devices remain a major obstacle for achieving practical quantum advantage. In the absence of fault-tolerant machines, ensemble learning, which combines multiple imperfect quantum devices, may unlock new quantum computing applications. Here, we introduce two ensemble-learning algorithms, namely Bagging and AdaBoost, for enhancing the accuracy of nonlinear regression problems. While Bagging trains variational quantum regressors independently, AdaBoost employs a sequential training strategy and achieves significantly better prediction accuracy. By incorporating systematic feature selection into our ensemble learning algorithms, we enhance their predictive power for highly nonlinear functions. We further improve prediction accuracy through a feature-to-qubit mapping strategy based on repeated feature encoding. The performance of our ensemble-based algorithms improves with both the number of regressors in the ensemble and the size of each regressor. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach using four benchmarking functions with varying degrees of nonlinearity by relying on a feature pool limited to first- and second-order polynomial terms.

For composers and performers, the agentic orchestra opens new possibilities for emergent, process-oriented musical creativity, offering a scalable foundation for quantum computer-aided music composition as quantum computing technology continues to advance.

Scaling universal quantum computation and implementing quantum machine learning with protected logical qubits remain challenging. To harness the computational potential of existing fault-tolerant quantum platforms, we introduce topological quantum reservoir computing, a novel approach using protected degrees of freedom for storage and the host system's intrinsic dynamics for information processing. Using a one-dimensional Kitaev chain, we show how long-lived Majorana edge modes and fast bulk responses support these complementary functions within the same many-body system. We identify the tradeoffs governing access to protected memory and show how matched interfaces enable programmable writing and interactions enable autonomous information consolidation from the bulk to the edge. These mechanisms improve performance on tasks requiring long-term retention of temporal information. Our results establish topology as a new resource for quantum reservoir computing and open a route toward near-term quantum computing and quantum machine learning.

Quantum annealing is a promising quantum algorithm for dealing with computationally hard optimization problems. Currently available hardware can implement the algorithm for a significant class of Hamiltonian problems. However, its performance is especially limited in discontinuous quantum phase transitions, where the energy gap shrinks exponentially with system size. In this paper, we introduce an extension of quantum annealing to a quantum neural network and study its performance in a number of prototypical optimization problems. In particular, we observe that already a random quantum annealing network of width W = 2 can exponentially reduce the number of evolution layers or depth of the annealing process in the quantum search problem. By tuning the network parameters the search problem can be solved with exponentially small errors by a quantum perceptron of depth L = 1 and width W = 2. The findings show that width of a quantum annealing network is critical to enhance the success probability of the algorithm by providing a larger and learnable evolution space for the annealing process.

Entanglement Lower Bounds for Symmetric Quantum Circuits

Davi Castro-Silva, Ryan Dancy, Sergii Strelchuk

Symmetric circuits require every symmetry of the input to extend to an automorphism of the circuit. In the classical setting, their connection to descriptive complexity yields strong unconditional lower bounds. We extend this approach to the quantum model of symmetric computation defined by Castro-Silva, Gur, and Strelchuk (ITCS 2026), proving a tradeoff between circuit size and entanglement width (the smallest $b$ such that, on every computational-basis input, the state after each layer factors into states on at most $b$ qubits each). Our main result identifies a graph property decidable in classical polynomial time that no polynomial-size family of symmetric quantum circuits on $n$-vertex graphs with entanglement width $o(n/\log n)$ can compute. The bound holds for circuits with arbitrary one-qubit and two-qubit gates and unrestricted depth, and remains valid under the weaker notion of symmetry with respect to even permutations of vertices. Our proof is based on a new quantum pebble game based on the partial trace. The resulting indistinguishability theorem transfers classical pebble-game lower bounds to quantum circuits with restricted entanglement. By contrast, without entanglement restrictions, we show that every graph property in BQP can be computed by a uniform family of polynomial-size symmetric quantum circuits. Restricting to circuits with no entanglement at all, we show that polynomial-size symmetric quantum circuits with entanglement width 1 can compute every graph property definable in fixed-point logic with counting. Finally, we give an efficient symmetric implementation of QAOA for MAX 3XOR, providing a starting point for studying entanglement lower bounds in quantum optimization algorithms.

QuFrame: Pauli Frame Tracking for Fault-Tolerant Quantum Control

Aleksandra Świerkowska, Ilgaz Er, Emmanouil Giortamis, Pramod Bhatotia

Fault-tolerant quantum computing requires continuous quantum error correction (QEC) to suppress physical noise. While Clifford-only workloads can defer corrections to software post-processing, universal computation breaks this paradigm: executing non-Clifford gates via magic-state gadgets introduces hard, real-time control-flow dependencies on error-corrected measurements. Consequently, the controller must extract syndromes, decode them, and update the Pauli frame in real time, closing the entire QEC loop within a strict $1\,μ\text{s}$ cycle. However, prior proposals on Pauli frame tracking remain largely theoretical, leaving the microarchitectural bottlenecks of tracking, resolving, and synchronizing frame updates across distributed control pipelines unaddressed. To this end, we present QuFrame, a control microarchitecture for real-time Pauli frame tracking that closes the QEC loop within $1\,μ\text{s}$ while sustaining QPU-rate throughput. By introducing QuFrameISA, we cleanly decouple instruction execution from continuous Pauli frame management. Execution is distributed across per-qubit Instruction Resolution Engines (IREs) that resolve instructions against the frame via a single table lookup, while a Pauli Frame Management Unit enables independent, lock-free concurrent commits. To strictly guarantee the $1\,μ\text{s}$ deadline, QuFrame pairs an asynchronous primary decoder with an on-chip fallback decoder that resolves in $144\,\text{ns}$, natively reconciling divergent updates in hardware without degrading logical error rates.

Fault-tolerant quantum processor with minimal external control

Christopher A. Pattison, Jeongwan Haah, John Preskill

Contemporary classical information processors integrate tens of billions of transistors printed on a chip, yet are fully controlled through comparatively few external wires. This is possible because data flows through hard-coded operations in step with a global clock and is steered by external instructions. Today's solid-state quantum processors are different -- every qubit receives instructions from outside the chip via its own dedicated control line, and error correction adds a further torrent of syndrome data that is transported off-chip. Here we show that the external control of a quantum chip can be greatly simplified. We propose a fault-tolerant architecture for universal quantum computation, inspired by modern classical processors, in which quantum information flows through a fixed, clocked device, propelled by local gates in a planar array. Our quantum processor receives an input stream of classical instructions via a small, constant number of external wires, each attached to a single qubit. Except for those few qubits coupled to external wires, all qubits evolve under a periodically-applied geometrically-local depth-3 circuit, which is fixed at the time the device is fabricated. Assuming a noiseless global clock (so that every operation is applied on schedule, though imperfectly), we establish an accuracy threshold theorem: For physical noise below a universal threshold -- afflicting every gate, measurement, and wire -- a chip with $n$ physical qubits can run any quantum algorithm that uses~$n/{\sf polylog}(n/ε)$ logical qubits, where $ε$ is the error rate per logical gate. Each logical gate takes time~$n/{\sf polylog}(n/ε)$; an alternative design, in which quantum operations remain strictly local and noisy, but on-chip wires broadcast noiseless classical signals instantaneously, reduces the time per logical gate to ${\sf polylog}(n/ε)$.

Gutzwiller Quantum-Classical Embedding for Dynamics Simulations of Correlated Electron Systems

Joshua Aftergood, Daniele Guerci, Jacob Brunton, Feng Zhang, Thomas Iadecola, Peter P. Orth

Simulating the real-time dynamics of correlated electron systems requires a nonperturbative treatment of local correlations over long times, a challenge for classical methods. We present the time-dependent Gutzwiller quantum-classical embedding framework, t-GQCE, for generic multi-orbital lattice models with time-dependent Hamiltonians. Within the time-dependent ghost Gutzwiller approximation, the lattice dynamics is mapped onto a noninteracting quasiparticle lattice, propagated classically, coupled to finite interacting embedding models propagated on quantum processors. The two parts exchange only embedding Hamiltonian parameters and single-particle density matrices, and the framework is agnostic to the quantum dynamics solver. We demonstrate it with adaptive variational quantum dynamics simulations of interaction quenches in the Hubbard model on the Bethe lattice. Without ghost orbitals, the embedding problem reduces to a single qubit, simulated on IBM quantum hardware with two qubits, including an ancilla. With more ghost orbitals, t-GQCE suppresses the artificial undamped oscillations of the conventional Gutzwiller approximation and approaches nonequilibrium dynamical mean-field theory results for weak and strong quenches. Statevector simulations with 2, 4 and 6 ghost orbitals per spin, or 8, 12 and 16 qubits, give adaptive circuits that follow the embedding states with infidelities below $5\times10^{-4}$. Under trapped-ion noise models, circuit compression, measurements in rotated orbital bases with post-selection, and noise mitigation reduce the measurement errors severalfold. At one third of the error rates of a hardware-benchmarked noise model, they reach an accuracy of $0.01$ for the single-particle density matrix and the double occupancy with $10^5$ shots per time step, and the Quantinuum H2-Emulator gives similar accuracy.

General Noise Channels and Mid-Circuit Measurements for Pre-Trajectory Sampling with Batched Execution

Louis Follet, Paavai Pari, Isidor Schoch, Thien Nguyen, Justin G. Lietz, Daniel Lowell

Many studies of quantum protocols require fast sampling of large amounts of measurement data from noisy quantum circuits, such as for training machine-learning decoders for quantum error correction. Exact simulation of realistic noise is expensive, while stabilizer-based simulators are fast but approximate, for example through Pauli twirling, which generally alters the output statistics. Pre-trajectory sampling with batched execution (PTSBE) accelerates exact trajectory simulation by sampling noise before state evolution. This method requires the outcome probability of every stochastic event to be independent of the state, which excludes mid-circuit measurements and general Kraus channels. We present a generalization of PTSBE to measurement and Kraus operators without approximation. Each event is partially pre-sampled, while deferred outcomes are drawn during grouped execution via multinomial branching and cached-state reuse. We implement the method for GPUs as an extension of CUDA-Q. On distance-3 topological code memories under amplitude damping, it is up to 24x faster than conventional trajectory sampling in CUDA-Q. On 30-qubit random circuits with mid-circuit measurements, it is up to 83x faster and outperforms the near-Clifford simulator Clifft when more than about 25% of the gates are non-Clifford. With repeated sampling of each final state, its sampling rate per trajectory exceeds that of CUDA-Q by nearly five orders of magnitude. Our method also allows us to compare exact amplitude damping with its Pauli-twirled channel on a surface-code memory, revealing that twirling overestimates the detection rate by 21% and raises the logical error rate of a lookup decoder by 8%. This work makes exact, high-throughput simulation practical for general noisy circuits, from data generation for training decoders to studying measurement-based fault-tolerant protocols under realistic noise.

Residual-Algebraic Design of Symmetry-Preserving Variational Quantum Circuits

Sahinur Reja

Symmetry-preserving (SP) variational quantum circuits restrict evolution to physically relevant sectors of Hilbert space, but symmetry preservation alone does not guarantee accessibility within those sectors. We show that a restricted generator set can possess a residual dynamical Lie algebra whose commutant further decomposes the target symmetry sector into invariant blocks. The associated block populations are conserved and impose a kinematic upper bound on the fidelity with a target state. We then invert this diagnostic into a circuit-design principle: candidate SP generators can be screened before variational optimization by asking whether they break the residual commutant, which invariant blocks they couple, and what adjoint module they generate under the residual algebra. For two orthogonal residual blocks we prove that a single elementary real inter-block rotation generates the full orthogonal algebra. We further derive a complex escape criterion: if the real and imaginary parts of an inter-block seed are independent over the residual intertwiner algebra, the generated module is the full complex coupling space and the enlarged algebra becomes special unitary. A connected residual-block graph yields a natural multi-block generalization and suggests minimum-cost spanning-tree circuit design. We demonstrate this design principle in the XXZ and Hubbard models, where residual-algebra screening identifies SP circuit extensions that remove the relevant accessibility obstructions. These examples establish a unified route from residual-symmetry diagnosis and fidelity bounds to predictive quantum circuit design.
