PADO-Pauli: A GPU-Accelerated Implementation of Pauli Propagation

Hyunwoo Kim, Youngseok Lee

Variational quantum algorithms utilize parameterized circuits many times during training, at new parameter values, over batches of data, and with gradients. Pauli propagation approximates the expectation values of such circuits classically by evolving the observable in the Heisenberg picture as a truncated sum of Pauli strings. We present PADO-Pauli, a GPU engine for Pauli propagation that compiles a circuit and its observables once into sparse matrices in which each rotation angle contributes only a cosine and a sine factor, so every later evaluation reuses the compiled program. It evaluates batches of parameters and data inputs embedded as rotation angles. By default its gradients come from a vector-Jacobian product registered with PyTorch's automatic differentiation, so they flow through the angles to any classical layer that produces them. PADO-Pauli also provides truncation by Pauli weight and coefficient size, and noise channels. Zero-filtering removes terms that do not contribute to an expectation value in the all-zero reference state and makes the batched forward pass about 1.5 times faster. Compared to cuPauliProp on an NVIDIA A100, PADO-Pauli evaluates up to 10 times faster on 4 to 12 qubits and uses up to 3.1 times less memory on 16 qubits. We check its accuracy against exact statevector simulation up to 28 qubits. These features make PADO-Pauli a practical toolkit for training variational quantum algorithms, including quantum machine learning models.

On the Computational Complexity of Blind Quantum Machine Learning

Rex Fleur, Tzula B. Propp, Sebastian Feld

Can quantum advantages in computational efficiency coexist with quantum advantages in privacy? In this paper, we construct and use MasonQ, a universal brickwork transpiler, to answer this question with a resounding yes. We approach this question by bridging two seemingly disparate paradigms: universal blind quantum computation (UBQC), based on measurement-based quantum computation, and quantum machine learning (QML) algorithms such as the Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd (HHL) algorithm and quantum recommendation systems. We provide new upper bounds on the depth of UBQC brickwork graphs, establishing that $D = \mathcal{O}(ωn)$, where $ω$ is the circuit width and $n$ its computational gate complexity. This is enabled by our newly developed transpiler MasonQ, which maps arbitrary quantum circuits to computational brickwork graph states. Given an optimal Qiskit circuit as input, we prove optimality (up to logarithmic corrections) of the MasonQ transpiler in terms of asymptotic scaling of the total brickwork graph width and depth after decomposition into bricks, enabling systematic cost analyses of blind implementations in near-term experiments and asymptotic regimes. Leveraging MasonQ, we derive depth bounds for several representative blind quantum machine learning (B-QML) algorithms, including the quantum Fourier transform, the HHL algorithm, recommendation systems, and quantum transformers. Lastly, we analyze concrete resource estimates for near-term demonstrations of blind quantum machine learning. We propose a minimal B-QML implementation requiring only a $12$ qubit quantum server. To the best of our knowledge, MasonQ is the first functional and optimal universal circuit-to-brickwork-graph transpiler to implement the full brickwork transpilation process from first principles.

Architecting Instruction Execution for Scalable Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computing

Dmitry Filippov, Justin Hogaboam, Mathias Soeken, Raghu Gatta, Prakash Murali, Matthias Troyer

Scaling fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) requires orchestrating millions of operations continuously across a tightly coupled classical-quantum control stack. A critical challenge in this orchestration is determining the optimal hardware-software boundary, i.e., the Quantum Instruction Set Architecture (QISA). We focus on cryogenic, fixed-connectivity qubit platforms, such as superconducting and topological Majorana qubits. For these, existing QISA choices either operate at too low a level, violating cryogenic thermal budgets at scale, or at too high a level, imposing unrealizable complexity on the control engine. To reason about these tradeoffs, we introduce the ARIES instruction set hierarchy, which categorizes QISAs into distinct abstraction levels. To systematically evaluate these levels, we develop an end-to-end evaluation toolflow featuring a cycle-accurate classical driver and hardware engine simulator. Using eight quantum applications across superconducting and topological qubit platforms, we measure instruction bandwidth, transceiver power dissipation, and measurement processing overheads. We demonstrate that while physical-level abstractions exceed the thermal limits of dilution refrigerators by orders of magnitude, canonical-level abstractions significantly underestimate hardware routing congestion and execution runtimes. Our Level 4 QISA (local logical operations) offers an optimal architectural balance. As quantum hardware platforms scale toward FTQC in the coming years, our findings provide concrete architectural guidance for the design of their classical control stacks and instruction interfaces.

A Controlled Null Result for Per-Gate Riemannian Optimization in VQE

Polina Belozerova, Pavel Osinenko

Parametrized gates in a variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) live on a product of unitary groups, which makes Riemannian optimization a natural candidate for improving training. We test this candidate in the clean per-gate setting: each single-qubit gate is optimized directly on U (2) and compared with Adam on an unconstrained Euler-angle chart representing the same physical gate, initialized from the same unitaries and tuned with a separate learning-rate sweep. On a validated hydrogen-chain benchmark, we find no measurable practical advantage from the Riemannian machinery. The direct manifold optimizer and the Euler-coordinate baseline are interleaved in final error and success rate; the classical coordinate baseline is not slower in iterations on the successful cells; and the choices of Riemannian metric, retraction, and momentum vector transport do not change the conclusion. We also identify four failure modes that can manufacture an apparent positive result: a trivial product-state benchmark, a projected-matrix Adam baseline that discards most of its own step, a non-unitary inherited Cayley retraction that lets the reported energy pass below the variational minimum, and a learning-rate grid whose optimum sits at its boundary. Each failure mode produced a plausible multi-seed result in our own workflow before being caught. The practical output of this paper is therefore a controlled null result and a validation checklist for VQE optimizer comparisons.

Principles and Techniques for Agentic Research in Experimental Physics

Niko R. Reed, Declan M. Daly, Andrew J. Beling, Jordan D. Giandrea, Ronald L. Walsworth, John W. Blanchard

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in physics research is increasingly common, yet few guidelines exist for its effective use. Starting with a principles-based approach, we outline a set of ten requirements for collaborative AI agents in experimental physics, spanning scientific norms, effective agent use, and domain expertise. We take a human-centered approach in which agents act as collaborators that amplify rather than replace the expertise of a human researcher (user) who directs the work and is responsible for its results. We then present a framework designed to enforce these requirements, detailing methods for improving off-the-shelf behavior of large language models (LLMs) with custom scaffolding and describing general-purpose scientific workflows and skills. In particular, we highlight the power of closed-loop campaigns where agents work iteratively toward a well-defined goal. We demonstrate the potential of this mode of research through six case studies in experimental physics, covering device design, hardware control, neural network optimization, physical model identification, and data analysis. All six case studies result in useful work products that add utility to our research, including a neural network architecture and solid-state quantum defect photodynamic model that substantially outperform our published work; a microwave waveguide design that extends a published design concept to a fabrication-ready device meeting our experimental constraints; a quantum control pulse sequence candidate that improves simulated performance by more than an order of magnitude; commissioning of an NMR spectrometer to $\sim$1~Hz frequency precision on live hardware; and finally a data-fitting pipeline that achieves, to our knowledge, the first reconstruction of the Ising microstate of an artificial spin-ice array from diffraction-limited magnetic imaging.

Deterministic and programmable generation of mechanical multipartite entangled states

Isabelle Franzen, Max-Emanuel Kern, Q. Rumman Rahman, Igor Kladarić, Stefano Marti, Yiwen Chu

Multimode mechanical resonators offer a large set of densely packed, long-lived bosonic modes within a single compact device, making them attractive for quantum simulation, quantum information processing, and quantum-enhanced sensing. Many such applications rely on multipartite entanglement, yet deterministic mechanical entanglement has so far been largely limited to pairs of modes. Here we demonstrate the deterministic and programmable preparation of multipartite entangled states among the phonon modes of a high-overtone bulk acoustic wave resonator coupled to a superconducting transmon qubit. By sequentially applying optimal-control-engineered isometries between the qubit and successive modes, we generate matrix product states of bond dimension two, including $W$, Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ), and cluster states. We fully characterize these states via quantum state tomography and certify genuine multipartite entanglement among up to four modes. We further demonstrate correlated-noise sensing by exploiting the complementary decoherence responses of the stored $W$ and GHZ states, isolating inter-mode phase correlations from local decoherence and placing quantitative bounds on collective dephasing. Our results establish a scalable approach to preparing multipartite entangled states in mechanical systems, with promising applications in multiparameter sensing and tests of quantum mechanics with massive objects.

Gaussian entanglement and quantum communication: geodesic optimization and multimode bounds

Hemant K. Mishra, Mark M. Wilde

Loss and noise limit the use of light to distribute entanglement, transmit quantum information, and establish secret keys. Gaussian states have a compact description in terms of means and covariance matrices, but their entanglement still requires global optimization. We develop a framework for computing Gaussian-restricted versions of the relative entropy of entanglement and Rains entanglement. An integral representation reveals a convex geometry under matrix geometric means and reduces Gaussian Rains entanglement to relative entropy minimized over Gaussian states with positive partial transpose. We obtain unique optimal reference states and additivity for independent Gaussian input states. Our convex-majorization algorithm has an explicit geometric convergence rate, uniform in the faithful regularization parameter and valid for pure input states. Controlled numerical integration and approximate convex subproblem solutions give a bit-complexity bound that is polynomial in accuracy bits for each fixed rational input covariance matrix, with explicit dependence on covariance size. These calculations bound extractable entanglement and, through the relative entropy of entanglement, extractable secret key. Teleportation simulation, energy allocation, and a convex optimization of passive-basis loss cores give bounds on quantum and private communication under an input-energy constraint, allowing arbitrary two-way classically assisted protocols. Two- and three-mode examples compare the bounds with achievable rates, including matching pure-loss benchmarks. Finally, we separate the Gaussian-restricted quantities from their unrestricted counterparts and give a photon-number truncation method with controlled error for computing the latter.

Scalable Exact Non-Pauli Noise Simulation for Fault-Tolerant Quantum Circuits

Yuewen Hou, Gokul Subramanian Ravi

Fault-tolerant quantum design relies on simulating noisy quantum error correction (QEC) circuits with hundreds to thousands of physical qubits and large circuit depths. High-throughput stabilizer simulators, such as Stim, are the de facto tools for this regime. However, these simulators typically support only Clifford gates and stochastic Pauli noise. As a result, more realistic device noise models-such as coherent control errors and thermal relaxation-are often approximated using Pauli Twirling. This replacement offers speed but can introduce inaccuracies. To address this, we propose QuasiStim for scalable exact QEC simulation of realistic noise, with QuasiFold as a compiler frontend and DeltaFrame as a sampling backend. QuasiFold lowers sampling overhead by merging equivalent non-Pauli sites in the quantum circuit. DeltaFrame samples the resulting stabilizer trajectories by reusing precomputed predicted references instead of resimulating the full tableau for each shot. QuasiFold can reduce the number of shots by over $100\times$ on bivariate-bicycle (BB) codes. Compared to a per-shot tableau simulator, DeltaFrame is $166\times$--$1331\times$ faster on rotated surface-code memory under coherent $R_Z$ noise as $d$ grows from 5 to 35. QuasiStim also makes the PTA bias measurable: the gap is non-negligible and varies with noise strength, decoder, and circuit schedule. QuasiStim will be open-sourced upon publication at https://github.com/isaachyw/QuasiStim

Sequential optimization for orbital-optimized variational quantum eigensolvers

Hiromichi Hayashi, Takeshi Sato

Orbital optimization within the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) framework is useful for improving active-space quality and accurately capturing electron correlation with shallow quantum circuits. The orbital-optimized unitary coupled cluster (OO-UCC) method [W. Mizukami et al., Phys. Rev. Res. 2, 033421 (2020)], in its original formulation, employs the orbital gradient and Hessian in a Newton--Laphson procedure. In this study, we propose sequential orbital-optimized unitary coupled cluster doubles (SOO-UCCD), extending the gradient-free sequential minimal optimization approach [K. M. Nakanishi et al., Phys. Rev. Res. 2, 043158 (2020)] to orbital rotation parameters. Based on the frequency structure implied by the algebraic properties of the orbital rotation generators, our method determines each orbital rotation angle from a nine-point energy profile. The energy landscape is evaluated by classically transforming the Hamiltonian integrals and contracting them with the reduced density matrices already measured for the VQE energy evaluation. This allows for orbital optimization without evaluating the orbital gradient or Hessian. By building on the core benefits of the OO-UCC framework, our method provides a gradient-free and hyperparameter-free orbital-optimization procedure. State-vector simulations for LiH in the STO-3G basis show that SOO-UCCD reproduces UCCSD-level accuracy along the bond-stretching curve and exhibits macro-iteration convergence comparable to that of L-BFGS-based orbital optimization.

Lifting: Quantum State Discrimination Framework for Mid-Circuit Measurements

Chi Zhang, Neel Vora, Yilun Xu, Gang Huang, Fan Chen, Lei Jiang

Quantum computing promises new capabilities beyond classical computation. Mid-circuit measurements (MCMs) enable error correction and outcome-dependent control, but their reliability is limited by noise on superconducting hardware. These operations depend on accurate quantum state discrimination (QSD), which assigns measured signals to state labels. Neural network-based (NN-based) QSD improves this assignment, yet its error remains bounded below by the Bayes risk of the available readout distributions. A complementary challenge is state deterioration as successive measurements accumulate and circuit execution advances, making static calibration less representative of the workload. We present Lifting, a QSD framework that addresses both challenges through repetition encoding, neural decoding, and in situ calibration. Encoding supplies joint in-phase and quadrature (I-Q) readout signals from the target and auxiliary qubits, while contextual calibration trains the decoder for the conditions encountered during execution. Our theoretical analysis shows that retaining additional channels from a fixed joint distribution cannot increase Bayes risk and establishes sufficient conditions for contraction of an overlap-based upper bound. Experiments across three IBM processors evaluate the framework on repeated stabilizer measurement, parity-check, and teleportation workloads.

In real settings, state preparation methods operate in stochastic scenarios, where the prepared state is represented as a mixed state, that is a probabilistic ensemble of pure states. Therefore, existing state preparation methods optimize a variational ansatz by averaging over all possible sequences of photon loss events contained in the prepared ensemble, yielding an output state which approximates the desired target with a certain average fidelity. Such an approach, however, does not take into account directly the distribution of fidelities across states in the ensemble. In this work, we aim to maximize the fidelity of only a subset of pure states sampled from the prepared ensemble, at the cost of reducing the average fidelity overall. This trade-off is relevant when the goal is to obtain high-fidelity states with a certain probability, rather than maximizing the average fidelity of the prepared ensemble. We borrow and adapt objective functions from risk-sensitive optimization, a framework that optimizes specific characteristics of a distribution rather than only its expectation, and that has been successfully applied in Reinforcement Learning. In the considered experimental settings, our results show that the infidelities of some pure states in the prepared ensemble can be reduced by up to four orders of magnitude when using risk-seeking objectives, compared to those achieved using the standard optimization of the average fidelity. Furthermore, we show that it is possible to control the trade-off between the probability of obtaining high-fidelity states and the lowest infidelity threshold reached, providing the flexibility to select the operating point that best suits a certain application.

A defect on a specific qubit severely restricts the connectivity between adjacent qubits, disrupts stabilizer measurements, induces frequent detection events, and consequently inflates the logical error rate. To mitigate this issue, various strategies have been proposed, such as syndrome extraction circuits based on stabilizer folding and the construction of super-stabilizers via gauge operators. In this work, we propose a defect-avoidance scheme tailored for the heavy-hexagon architecture, which features a lower connectivity of 2 to 3. We experimentally validate this approach on IBM quantum hardware by identifying physical defects and implementing the defect-avoidance circuits. As a result, avoiding the defect yields an approximately 10\% reduction in the logical error rate per cycle for both logical $X$ and $Z$ errors. These findings demonstrate that defects in superconducting hardware can be successfully handled even under the constrained connectivity of heavy-hexagon lattices, and that defect avoidance substantially reduces logical errors on real quantum hardware.

An independent construction on middle quantum Reed--Muller codes yields C${}^r$Z gates that saturate the equal-syndrome weight bound. These results establish stabilizer syndromes as a constructive link between the stabilizer structure of a quantum code, the logical computations it can directly support, and the fault-tolerance properties of their physical realizations.
