Weight-Space Programming of Variational Quantum Circuits: Large Language Models Write Quantum Circuit Parameters Without an Optimizer

Debarshi Kundu, Swaroop Ghosh, Vasant G Honavar

Variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) optimize circuit angles through repeated quantum-device evaluations, often facing vanishing gradients and high optimization costs. We ask whether a large language model (LLM) can write these angles directly. Extending *weight-space programming* from classical networks to quantum circuits, we provide a frontier LLM (\gpt) with the problem, circuit, and gate conventions. Its angles are executed exactly as written, without returning losses, gradients, or accuracy feedback. Across 139 requests, we test handwritten-digit classification, VQE ground-state estimation for spin chains and H$_2$, and MaxCut QAOA, comparing against random weights, classical optimization, and optimization initialized with LLM weights on the same circuit. When parameters have interpretable structure, the LLM matches or approaches the optimizer: it achieves 95--100\% of the energy spectrum on every VQE problem, derives the exact H$_2$ ground state in closed form, finds optimal depth-one QAOA angles for every graph, and reaches 93--96\% accuracy on digits 3 versus 8 with 12--18 hand-written weights, outperforming an optimizer trained on the same ten examples. Its weights are also strong warm starts: optimization reaches its best value after a median of one evaluation rather than hundreds. Allowed to design circuits, the LLM chooses data-dependent rotation chains encoding pixel importance in all 30 cases (one qubit for binary tasks, one chain per qubit for ten classes), reaching 65\% accuracy across ten digits. Yet the approach fails in opaque weight spaces: accuracy drops to chance when fixed circuits exceed 30 weights, while optimization improves. It is also sensitive to gate conventions: 6 of 42 QAOA replies reverse mixing-angle signs and fail. We release all prompts, replies, and scores.

Adaptive Policy-Guided Error Mitigation (APGEM): A Unified Learning-Based Framework for Noise Resilience in Quantum Machine Learning

Bisma Majid, Shabir Ahmad Sofi, Mir Mohammad Yousuf

Error mitigation stands between variational quantum algorithms and useful results on today's noisy hardware, yet in practice a mitigation method is usually chosen once, by hand, and applied unchanged for the life of an experiment. This is a poor fit for quantum machine learning (QML), where the circuit and the noise drift as training proceeds, so the best strategy in one regime is often wrong in another. We present a generalized form of Adaptive Policy-Guided Error Mitigation (APGEM), which treats the choice of mitigation technique as an online decision made from an algorithm's own feedback. A single contextual-bandit controller observes signals any variational algorithm already produces, such as fidelity, entropy, reward or loss, and gradient behavior, and selects among Zero-Noise Extrapolation, Probabilistic Error Cancellation, Measurement Error Mitigation, Dynamical Decoupling, and circuit optimization per step. Because it depends only on this shared feedback interface, the framework applies unchanged across supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement, and hybrid quantum-classical learning. Evaluated on five paradigms across 80 algorithm-and-noise conditions, adaptive selection reaches 95.5% of an oracle that always picks the best technique and significantly outperforms all six fixed strategies (Friedman $χ^{2}=92.18$, $p\approx1.1\times10^{-17}$; pairwise Wilcoxon $p<4\times10^{-6}$). The learned policy is noise-aware: its favored technique shifts as depolarizing and readout error rise. On noise models from eight real IBM devices, APGEM tracks the oracle almost exactly (99.9% of oracle utility) at a flat cost of roughly $1.3\times10^{4}$ shots from three to eight qubits.

The energetic cost of loading classical data in quantum computers

Joao F. Doriguello, Alessandro Luongo, Harshit Verma

Quantum algorithms for machine learning, chemistry, and cryptanalysis often assume coherent access to classical data at negligible physical cost. We instead treat data loading as a hardware process and quantify the dynamic energetic cost of a quantum random access memory (QRAM) query on a superconducting processor. Using the metric-noise-resource framework, we relate query fidelity and power to gate duration, relative two-qubit drive strength, and the distribution of attenuation across a multi-stage refrigerator, while accounting for thermal photons and gate errors. We analyze the linear-depth multiplexer and the noise-resilient bucket-brigade architectures, adapting the latter to a native superconducting gate set and refining its fidelity analysis using the fully decomposed gate schedule. For a representative bucket-brigade QRAM query with a $12$-qubit address register we obtain a query fidelity of $0.969$. The query consumes $3.51\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J}$ of dynamic energy, corresponding to an average dynamic power of $4.38\,\mathrm{W}$ and a peak of $64.4\,\mathrm{W}$. Our optimization shows that, at fixed target fidelity, the operating point and attenuation profile can change the average power by a factor of two or more.

Invariance can prevent overfitting and shortcut learning in Quantum Neural Networks

Giuseppe D'Ambruoso, Giovanni Gramegna, Domenico Pomarico, Giuseppe Magnifico, Cosmo Lupo

Many image classification tasks have labels that are unchanged by rotations and reflections. Equivariant quantum neural networks incorporate such symmetries by design, but the specific benefit of symmetry is difficult to isolate. Equivariant quantum circuits are usually compared with architectures that also differ in gates, structure or size. Here, we investigate the role of invariance in a controlled setting, classifying images whose labels are invariant under the dihedral group $D_4$. We compare four eight-qubit models with identical encoding, circuit structure and number of trainable parameters, and equivalent dynamical Lie algebras, and benchmark invariant implementations against non-invariant ones in terms of generalization error, robustness to shortcut learning and resilience to a realistic noise model. On four binary tasks, including MNIST and two scientific data sets of galaxies and red blood cells, the invariant models trained on a few tens of images show better generalization than non-invariant ones. Invariance also makes the models immune to orientation shortcuts: when trained with a class-dependent corner watermark, the non-invariant models learn the watermark and often drop to chance level when it is moved. We show that the gain in accuracy of invariant models persists also under a realistic noise model.

Machine-learning decoders for fusion-based quantum computing in real time

Daniel Barter, Jacob C. Bridgeman, Chris Dawson, Terry Farrelly, Shane Fleming, Mercedes Gimeno-Segovia

Fault-tolerant quantum computation requires decoders that combine accuracy, throughput, low latency, and scalability to large numbers of physical qubits. In photonic fusion-based architectures, erasures from photon loss and probabilistic fusion measurements are a dominant error mechanism, motivating decoders that remain accurate at high erasure rates. We introduce PRISM, a transformer-based machine-learning decoder designed to exploit the known locations of erasures in protocols subject to both Pauli and erasure errors. We benchmark PRISM across a wide range of fault-tolerant protocols based on surface codes, color codes, and quantum low-density parity-check codes, with distances up to 24. Across these benchmarks, PRISM matches the logical error rates of integer-programming-based decoders while being significantly faster, including at error rates up to 50% of threshold. We further optimize the decoder and its real-time hardware implementation to reduce latency while maintaining accuracy. Our optimized implementation on an AMD VP1902 FPGA can decode logical blocks of distance 8 in both space and time, demonstrating a latency of 6.59 microseconds and an amortized decoding time of 1.56 microseconds per logical block is possible. These results show that machine-learning decoding can provide accurate, low-latency inference for erasure-dominated fault-tolerant quantum architectures.

A Hardware-Trained Generative Quantum Eigensolver for Sample-Based Quantum Diagonalization of Organotin Hydroxides up to 70 Qubits

Kunal Kumar, Kimberlee Keithley, Toshiya Iwamori, Shu Kanno, Ikko Hamamura, Takao Kobayashi

Quantum hardware can provide feedback for training circuit generators beyond the reach of exact classical simulation. We train generative quantum eigensolver (GQE) policies, transformers conditioned on molecular descriptors, to generate the state-preparation circuits for sample-based quantum diagonalization (SQD). Training runs first on a noiseless simulator and then with the IBM Heron processor ibm_kobe inside the loop, at 46 and 70 qubits ($1.2 \times 10^8$ shots for the four main training runs). A classical CCSD(T) reference energy of the training molecules enters only an admission gate and two auxiliary reward terms. During hardware training, a reward penalty prevents collapse to circuits that sample only the Hartree-Fock determinant. Policies trained on transition-metal complexes are applied without retraining to Sn(OH)$_3$-Me and its hydrates, minimal models of organotin photoresists for extreme-ultraviolet lithography, from 32 to 70 qubits. After one round of hardware sampling, followed by classical ranking and recovery of the cached counts with the molecular Hamiltonian and no further QPU calls, 27 of 28 runs at 46-70 qubits are within 15 mHa of the density-matrix renormalization group (DMRG) reference (median 4.5 mHa), and in all eight runs with an untrained control the trained policy is more accurate. At 56-70 qubits the selected circuits use 34-72 times fewer two-qubit gates than the local unitary cluster Jastrow (LUCJ) circuit transpiled for the same device. These results demonstrate that circuit generators can be trained with quantum hardware at 70 qubits.

Normalizing Flows for Classical Simulation of Quantum Circuits in Phase Space

Edward Jiang, Timothy Heightman, Marcin Płodzień

We present a novel framework to learn a representation of quantum circuits based on the phase space formulation of many-body systems. Given that $N$-qubit states can be represented as probability distributions on a smooth $2N$-dimensional manifold, we show how unitary gates can be parameterized as normalizing flows between these distributions, opening a new avenue for quantum circuit simulation using generative models. We evaluate our method on three common families of quantum circuits, and show that its accuracy exceeds that of current state-of-the-art neural network methods for large system sizes.

Analog quantum approximate optimization algorithm for solving the weighted MaxCut problem on three-dimensional single-atom Rydberg array

Junxi Chen, I. I. Beterov

We theoretically investigate an analog implementation of the quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA) for the weighted maximum cut problem (MaxCut) problem. The protocol uses a three-dimensional array of individually trapped Rydberg atoms driven by globally addressed laser pulses, with site-dependent detunings applied near the Rydberg resonance. As benchmarks, we consider twenty randomly weighted 3-regular graphs with 12 vertices each. Problem weights are encoded directly in the interatomic interaction energies by choosing appropriate three-dimensional atom positions. We compare two parameter-optimization strategies: discretized quantum annealing and warm-start initialization using a graph neural network (GNN). The combined optimization strategy creates a portfolio that outperforms the Goemans-Williamson (GW) rounding almost in all instances in the noiseless case. We analyze the sampling statistics and the total pulse-time cost of both approaches. Finally, we emulate the performance of the optimized schedules on quantum trajectories using a realistic error model of the Aquila neutral-atom quantum processor.

Dynamical GKP codes

Jonathan Pelletier, Baptiste Royer

Dynamical codes protect quantum information with sequences of measurements, generalizing the stabilizer formalism of static codes. However, the dynamical formalism has been developed exclusively for qubit systems. While Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) codes are promising candidates for quantum computing that encode qubits in the continuous phase space of oscillators, no dynamical formalism has been developed for them. In this work, we bridge this gap and introduce dynamical GKP codes, combining continuous-variable lattice encodings with time-dependent translation measurement dynamics. To obtain the update rules and the distance of a given measurement sequence, we first generalize GKP codes to subsystem GKP codes using scaled symplectic lattices. Building on this structure, we adapt the ancestry, detector, and benign-error machinery originally developed for dynamical qubit codes. From this construction, we obtain a general and efficient method to compute the syndrome and distance of a dynamical GKP code from a persistent stabilizer lattice and a bounded inference window. We illustrate the framework with the example of the two-mode square-hexagonal Floquet GKP code. This code has reduced measurement weight compared to both the square and hexagonal GKP codes, while combining their set of logical gates, resulting in easier implementation of the full single-qubit Clifford group. Finally, we show that measurements with irrational symplectic overlap, which have no analog in discrete qubit codes, reduce to destructive homodyne detection in the single-mode case. These results extend an important qubit-code tool to continuous-variable systems and identify a new bridge between discrete stabilizer measurements and continuous homodyne detection.

Quantum-Informed Graph Decomposition

Matthew Brunet, Leah Prepchuk, Rebekah Herrman

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) is a near-term quantum algorithm for solving combinatorial optimization problems, but its accuracy and resource requirements may scale poorly with problem size or structure, reducing its utility in practice. Approaches such as the Graph Decomposition for Optimization Algorithm (GDOA) seek to address this by iteratively reducing an input problem until it reaches a size where it can be solved using QAOA while maintaining the same optimal solution. This algorithm, however, requires solving integer programs that have exponentially many variables, and can result in suboptimal solutions, as the integer programs may contain slack terms. In this work, we propose a heuristic called the Quantum Enhanced Graph Decomposition (QEGD) algorithm. Its novelty lies in its use of QAOA as a subroutine for sparsifying a graph through local vertex-merging. The algorithm, when used to sparsify MaxCut problems that are then solved using QAOA, achieves higher approximation ratios across all tested graphs than GDOA for random 5-regular and 6-regular graphs. We then compare the time-to-solution and approximation ratio of QEGD to other QAOA variants a collection of Erdös-Rényi graphs. QEGD has higher approximation ratios on average than QAOA$^2$ and ParaQAOA on all tested graphs. It also results in higher average approximation ratios than DC-QAOA on sparse Erdös-Rényi graphs and comparable average approximation ratio on denser Erdös-Rényi graphs. However, its average wall-clock time is between 0.4\% and 5\% of the average DC-QAOA wall-clock time.

A Compiler for Fault Tolerant Quantum Computing

Narasinga Rao Miniskar, Elaine Wong, Seyong Lee, Vicente Leyton-Ortega, Jeffrey S. Vetter, Travis S. Humble

Fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) needs compiler intermediate representations (IRs) that preserve error-correcting-code choices, syndrome information, and non-Clifford resource requirements through analysis, simulation, and hardware lowering. We report practical experience building FTQC, an open-source Multi-Level Intermediate Representation (MLIR) dialect that makes logical qubits, syndromes, magic states, and Pauli frames typed static single assignment (SSA) values. Its error-correction-aware type system, two-level lowering design, and IR-resident resource analysis provide one inspectable pipeline to Open Quantum Assembly Language~3 (OpenQASM~3), the Quantum Intermediate Representation (QIR), Stim, and IQM hardware. Case studies across three code families show that typed metadata makes code-specific decisions verifiable and retargetable: on the 20-qubit Pathfinder at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, decoding reduced logical readout error by 3.3 times. They also expose hidden architectural costs: for a three-qubit Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT-3), a magic-state factory requires five times the peak qubits of the encoded circuit. These experiences demonstrate a practical foundation for developing and evaluating fault-tolerant compiler transformations across software and hardware targets.

Quantum simulation of real-time bosonic dynamics with binary and unary position encodings on superconducting and trapped-ion processors

Masanori Hanada, Jiwoo Seo, Seok-Kyun Son, Junggi Yoon

Simulating a bosonic degree of freedom on qubit hardware requires discretizing its position space, and the qubit encoding of the resulting lattice determines the circuits that realize its real-time dynamics. We compare binary and unary encodings of a position lattice on superconducting (IBM) and trapped-ion (IonQ) processors. Binary encoding applies the kinetic term through the quantum Fourier transform; unary encoding uses one qubit per site, single-qubit rotations for the potential and excitation-conserving exchange gates for the kinetic term. For a single degree of freedom the unary dynamics is a free-fermion problem, and its product formula compresses exactly into a nearest-neighbor circuit of fixed size. With about two hundred two-qubit gates, these circuits resolve tunneling in a symmetric double well on both processors, with the right-well probability falling from 0.97 to 0.06 on IBM and from 0.96 to 0.12 on IonQ, whereas binary product-formula circuits with several thousand two-qubit gates do not reproduce the transfer. Noiseless emulation indicates that this contrast reflects the compilation as well as the encoding: without the fixed-size optimization, a unary product formula that captures the transfer also requires several thousand two-qubit gates. Post-selection on the single-excitation subspace removes leakage, and because every energy eigenstate of the symmetric grid populates both wells equally, the measured population reversal bounds the coherence between energy eigenstates from below. Coupled-oscillator benchmarks, where inter-register interactions prevent this exact compression, show that the preferred encoding depends on the grid size and the evolution time.

Towards large-scale ab initio Molecular Dynamics Simulation with Efficient Sampling-based Quantum Computing Algorithms

Ankit Khandelwal, Nirmal M R, Aaryav Mishra, Debasmita Bhoumik, Dhinakaran Vinayagamurthy, M Girish Chandra

Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) requires the calculation of highly accurate electronic-structure energies and nuclear forces at every time step, which can quickly become computationally expensive. Sample-based quantum diagonalization offers a route to such calculations on quantum hardware, but its use in AIMD simulations has so far been limited to active spaces of about 20 qubits. In these spaces, exact diagonalization is tractable, and the quantum circuits are typically built from standard coupled-cluster amplitudes. Here, we introduce SqDRIFT-AIMD, which combines randomized sample-based Krylov quantum diagonalization (SqDRIFT) with analytical nuclear gradients. With SqDRIFT-AIMD, we obtain more accurate ground-state energies than the conventional coupled-cluster singles and doubles (CCSD) driven AIMD simulation for the $40$-qubit active space. We apply SqDRIFT-AIMD to study the dynamics of proton transfer in the Zundel cation, using circuits executed on IBM Heron r3 quantum processors. SqDRIFT energies fall below CCSD across the $40$-qubit double-well PES and stay within a few m$E_\text{h}$ of CCSD at 52 qubits. The $40$-qubit SqDRIFT-AIMD trajectory tracks the SHCI-driven proton transfer to within $1.33$ mÅ and exhibits excellent total energy conservation, with a standard deviation of only $0.245$ m$E_\text{h}$. These results extend quantum-centric AIMD to active spaces beyond the reach of exact diagonalization.

Nonperturbative string dynamics in a (2+1)D lattice gauge theory on a superconducting quantum processor

Sanziana Stelea, Anthony Gandon, Francesco Tacchino, Jad C. Halimeh, Ivano Tavernelli

Electric flux strings underpin confinement in gauge theories. In two spatial dimensions, their evolution involves competing processes of charge motion, pair creation, and transverse deformation. Accessing these dynamics beyond perturbative limits challenges classical approaches, as entanglement growth restricts the times accessible to tensor-network simulations. Here we use quantum simulation to investigate a (2+1)D Z2 lattice gauge theory with dynamical matter and an explicit magnetic plaquette interaction, spanning strongly confined and nonperturbative deconfined regimes. Using 63 superconducting qubits, including 12 ancillas, we evolve initially localized strings with static or dynamical endpoint charges. Spatially resolved measurements reveal rapid charge and flux spreading in the deconfined regime, enhanced matter occupation in the string interior near the pair-creation resonance, and suppressed spreading at strong electric fields. String-path occupations further probe transverse rearrangements between configurations connected by plaquette operations. Retaining separate matter and gauge degrees of freedom makes local Gauss-law constraints directly accessible for error detection. Combining these constraints with ancilla checks and noise rescaling, we measure dynamics in circuits containing up to 15 second-order Trotter steps. Our results establish quantum simulation as a practical approach to exploring nonperturbative string dynamics in two spatial dimensions, with gauge redundancy serving as an experimental resource.

Simulating closed- and open-system dynamics of Tavis--Cummings lattices on fault-tolerant quantum computers

Soumya Sarkar, Rakshit Gharat, Nam Nguyen, Gavin Brennen

Tavis--Cummings lattices couple cavities containing several emitters and host polaritonic many-body dynamics whose real-time correlations are costly to compute classically in two dimensions, especially when unequal emitter relaxation or dephasing rates break permutation symmetry. We analyze logical resources for fault-tolerant closed- and open-system simulation of an atom-resolved lattice with $L$ cavities, $N$ emitters per cavity, and photon cutoff $n\_{\max}$, including target observable estimation. Building on ladder-operator block encoding, we construct circuits for photon hopping and local light--matter exchange and combine them to encode the lattice Hamiltonian. By updating source and destination photon occupations in place, the hopping circuit eliminates the auxiliary $L\lceil\log_2(n\_{\max}+1)\rceil$-qubit photon register used by two-register implementations. Across three uniform-coefficient examples with $16$ to $256$ cavities, the controlled walk uses $4.75$--$8.11%$ fewer Toffoli gates than the paired two-register implementation under matched shared preparation, precision, control, and workspace conventions. For closed systems, we implement real-time evolution with generalized quantum signal processing and bound the cost of estimating retarded cavity spectral functions. For open systems with photon loss, emitter relaxation, and dephasing, we use a sampled first-order dilation and bound the runs and timesteps needed to estimate transient photon statistics such as $g^{(2)}(0)$. With state-preparation and measurement costs explicit, these estimates provide a reference for assessing fault-tolerant light--matter lattice simulation costs.

Pool-free, residual-triggered ansatz growth for variational imaginary-time evolution: when a fixed operator order beats adaptive screening

Yu-Hao Chen, Renata Wong

Adaptive variational algorithms such as ADAPT-VQE and AVQITE screen an operator pool at every growth step, and ADAPT-VQE also re-optimizes all parameters. We study a pool-free alternative for variational imaginary-time evolution (VITE). The layers are fixed in advance by nested commutators of the Hamiltonian, grouped by symmetry and particle number, and the next layer is appended when the relative McLachlan residual exceeds a threshold. On five non-integrable systems (the mixed-field Ising chain and tori, and stretched H$_4$), this dynamic VITE reaches a relative error of $10^{-4}$ with 1.6-5.4 times fewer circuits than the cheapest of six to eight adaptive variants, including single-Pauli-string and qubit-excitation pools and batched ADAPT-VQE. On the chain the advantage persists from 8 to 16 sites. Screening and re-optimization account for most of the adaptive methods' cost. On the spin models, a longitudinal-field scan shows that the advantage requires broken integrability, which makes adaptive screening expensive. The advantage is lost on transverse-field Ising models, at tight targets (relative error $10^{-6}$) and where the schedule front-loads weak layers.

Cross-Platform Benchmarking of GHZ-State Generation in Quantum Processors via Cloud Services

Sergio Hernández, Unai Aseguinolaza, David Aguirre, Marta Gili, Joseba Hernández, Guillermo Valverde

Quantum computing has rapidly evolved into a diverse ecosystem of hardware platforms that differ in their underlying technologies and operational characteristics. Consequently, benchmarking has become essential for identifying the strengths of quantum processors, comparing their performance, and enabling informed decisions when selecting the most appropriate quantum computing platform for specific applications. In this work, we benchmark nine commercially accessible quantum processors from five vendors, spanning superconducting and trapped-ion technologies, using the multipartite entanglement benchmark from the QuSquare suite. All experiments were performed through remote cloud-access services, with the default compilation of each provider and without error mitigation, reflecting the performance experienced by end users. The benchmark estimates the fidelity of Greenberger--Horne--Zeilinger (GHZ) states by direct fidelity estimation and searches for the largest GHZ state whose fidelity is compatible, within the statistical error, with the genuine multipartite entanglement (GME) threshold $F=1/2$. Our results reveal a substantial gap between the total number of physical qubits available on a processor and the size of the largest GHZ state passing the benchmark: the best superconducting processors reach $N^*=27$ and $N^*=24$ qubits, less than a quarter of their registers, whereas the 12-qubit trapped-ion processor passes with its full register. We further show that median calibration data are poor predictors of the benchmark outcome, and that the reported sizes depend strongly on the statistical decision rule: requiring certified GME reduces the number of devices that succeed at any tested size from nine to four. These results provide a baseline for future cross-platform benchmarking of quantum processors.

Quantized memory of measurement records near the light cone of quantum circuits

Jamal Slim

Quantum processors now measure qubits in the middle of a circuit, and the stream of outcomes is the raw output of error correction, measurement-based state preparation and monitored dynamics. For a scrambling circuit, predicting the statistics of this stream is as hard as simulating the processor, so there is no exact reference to check it against once the device outgrows classical simulation. We show that near the light cone the problem becomes exactly solvable, for any two-qubit gate. In a brickwork circuit with one polarized qubit in a maximally mixed background, measurements along the edge of the light cone form a two-state Markov chain, and measurements $d$ sites inside the edge are generated by $\lfloor d/2\rfloor+1$ qubits that move with it, while the rest of the chain, however large, acts on them as a memoryless bath. The memory of the record is therefore quantized, one bit on the edge and at most one more for every site of displacement, a bound that generic gates attain. The same causal structure determines which hardware noise the record can see, removes that sensitivity at no gate cost, and yields a tomography-free witness of dual-unitarity. On a 156-qubit superconducting processor, a demonstration with its analysis specified in advance finds the predicted memory one and two sites inside the light cone, and none where the theory forbids it. This gives dynamic circuits an exact reference at any system size, and shows that the collision models of open-system theory arise exactly inside chaotic dynamics.

Frustration-free control for quantum-state preparation: an exact first-passage theory

T. Dörstel, T. C. Lang, T. Iadecola, J. H. Wilson, M. Buchhold

Quantum circuits based on local measurements and feedback can autonomously prepare and stabilize entangled many-body states. In frustration-free control, the target manifold is specified by local constraints, whose violations are detected by measurement and removed by feedback. We develop an exact theory of this mechanism for the preparation of Dicke states of $N_q$ qubits. Dicke states span the permutation-symmetric subspace, one per magnetization sector. Local singlet projectors define the constraints and detect departures from permutation symmetry. The resulting dynamics closes exactly on the span of the $N_q(N_q-1)/2$ singlet-pair projectors and is equivalent to a classical first-passage process: measurements move the pair endpoints, while feedback absorbs pairs on measured bonds. The closure reduces the exponentially large many-body Liouvillian to a generator of polynomial size. In one dimension we solve this process in closed form. Its slowest rate, $2[1-\cos(π/N_q)]$ in units of the measurement rate, is an exact Liouvillian eigenvalue. For any scheme of this class, the relaxation time is bounded below by the inverse fraction of pair configurations on which feedback acts; local feedback reaches $O(N_q)$ of $O(N_q^2)$ configurations, requiring a time at least linear in $N_q$. A second exact closure determines the final state reached from any initial condition. Extensions to modified transport, restricted feedback, and physical errors yield an exact transport-to-absorber crossover, geometric preparation laws, and a bound on the stationary leakage. An exact second-moment closure determines the ensemble fluctuations: the measurement noise is conserving, while feedback generates removal shot noise and pair correlations. Preparation, acceleration, robustness, and fluctuations are thus governed by one exactly solvable process: the transport and absorption of local constraint violations.

A Universal Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computing Architecture with Globally Driven Atom Arrays

Zhihao Zhang, Shuaifan Cao, Xinyang Yu, Yanyan Chen, Yueyang Min, Yingzhou Li

Scalable quantum computation requires reconciling programmability with simple, high-fidelity control. Here, we propose a universal fault-tolerant architecture using globally driven dual-species atom arrays. In a hybrid neutral-atom system, interspecies Rydberg interactions enable qubit addressability without locally addressed quantum gates. We construct a universal native instruction set, Global Rydberg Atom Instruction Language (GRAIL), and show that crosstalk errors can be suppressed to the $10^{-4}$ level. We compile surface-code error correction and magic state cultivation into GRAIL, and parallelize the compilation to suppress crosstalk. Under a hardware-inferred circuit-level noise model, we obtain an error-correction threshold of $1.43\%$ for the resulting quantum memory and a logical error rate below $10^{-9}$ for magic states cultivated at an experimentally accessible Rydberg Rabi frequency. By replacing local gate addressing with atom-mediated selectivity, this architecture removes the associated calibration requirements, offering a route to simpler control for large-scale fault-tolerant neutral-atom computation.
