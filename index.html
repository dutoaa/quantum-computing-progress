Cqlib: A High-Performance Software Stack for Quantum Computing

Tianyan Quantum Group

This paper presents Cqlib, a high-performance software stack for quantum computing built around a unified quantum circuit model. Its native Rust core provides efficient implementations of circuit processing, quantum-state and operator computation, compilation, and optimization, while Python and C interfaces support algorithm development and system integration. Cqlib covers quantum program construction, simulation, analysis, compilation, device adaptation, and result processing under consistent program semantics. Through OpenQASM and the Quantum Control Instruction Set (QCIS), Cqlib supports program representation and hardware-oriented execution. Cqlib further provides backend interfaces for device execution, with Cqlib-Tianyan serving as one implementation for QCIS-compatible quantum hardware. Evaluations using parameterized compilation tasks from Benchpress and related tests show that Cqlib achieves good functional coverage, compilation correctness, and execution efficiency within the evaluated task scope. We develop Cqlib-Algorithm as a modular library of reusable operators and composable components for widely used quantum algorithms, including quantum search, phase estimation, and variational algorithms, with additional support for error mitigation. To complement these building blocks with domain-specific workflows, we further develop Cqlib-VQE, Cqlib-QAOA, and Cqlib-QML for application-level studies in quantum chemistry, combinatorial optimization, and quantum machine learning, respectively. Cqlib also integrates source-grounded Skills and MCP-based tools to support agent-assisted program generation, backend execution, and result-guided workflow refinement. Overall, Cqlib provides a unified and extensible software foundation that connects quantum algorithm development, simulation, compilation, and execution on QCIS-compatible quantum hardware.

Machine Learning-Based Quantum Error Mitigation for Bound-State Simulations

Sumit Choudhary, Tarunpreet Kaur, Harjeet Kaur, Harpreet Singh

Noise in quantum devices remains a major challenge for reliable quantum simulations, making quantum error mitigation an important tool for improving their accuracy. Machine learning-based quantum error mitigation (ML-QEM) provides a useful approach, but its performance depends on the training datasets and may produce reconstructed quantum states that are not physically valid. In this work, we introduce an ML-QEM framework that uses residual learning with convex projection to ensure that reconstructed density matrices satisfy physically valid density matrices. We study the effect of different training datasets and regression models using Haar-random and variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) ansatz states. We compare the proposed approach with conventional ML-QEM, readout error mitigation, and zero-noise extrapolation. The method is tested on IBM quantum hardware for three- and four-qubit simulations of the bound $Λ$-deuteron and $Λ$-$α$ hypernuclear systems, respectively. For the three-qubit system, ridge regression trained on VQE-ansatz states gives a ground-state energy of -0.134 MeV, close to the ideal value of -0.135 MeV, with a state fidelity of 0.999. For four-qubit system, kernel ridge regression gives ground-state energies of -3.014 and -3.045 MeV, with corresponding state fidelities of 0.995 and 0.998. These results show that physically constrained reconstruction, along with the choice of training datasets and regression models, can improve ML-QEM performance for the quantum simulations studied in this work.

Our work not only proposes a quantum large model that can significantly reduce the computational cost, but also promotes the quantum computation towards general computing.

Observable-Level Expressivity in Fixed-Readout Quantum Neural Networks

Iqra Bashir, Yuanfeng Wang, Mingda Huang, Keren Li, Qi Qin

A central question in quantum machine learning is which functions of an input state a model can represent when its measurement is fixed by hardware or by experimental design. We address this question for quantum neural networks with a fixed readout observable, where the trainable pre-measurement dynamics determine the effective observable in the Heisenberg picture and the scalar output is the measured expectation value itself, without trainable affine post-processing. We characterize the reachable effective observables under three physical resource classes: unitary evolution, which preserves the spectrum of the readout; mixed-unitary evolution, which relaxes this to a majorization constraint while preserving the trace; and completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) evolution, whose Heisenberg adjoint is unital rather than trace-preserving and therefore lifts the trace constraint inherited at the mixed-unitary level. The three reachable sets are a fixed-spectrum orbit, its convex hull, and an operator interval, and the inclusions are strict for any readout that is not proportional to the identity. The separation is witnessed locally: for a fixed Pauli readout $Z_j$ on an arbitrary number of qubits, the amplitude-damping observable $γ\id+(1-γ)Z_j$ lies outside the mixed-unitary level for every $γ>0$. We realize the CPTP level with a Stinespring-channelized circuit and derive closed-form loss floors, $4γ^2/3$ and $γ^2$ for the unitary and mixed-unitary levels and zero at the CPTP level, which the numerics reproduce within precision. Structured-observable, Rényi-moment and classification experiments illustrate how the hierarchy manifests in finite-depth ansätze. The results identify which spectral constraint each physical resource relaxes, framing reachable-observable classes as a resource for expressivity rather than an architectural advantage.

Thermal state preparation with shallow quantum circuits

Cenk Tüysüz, Simon Deconihout, Enrico Rinaldi, Michele Grossi

Preparing thermal states on noisy quantum hardware requires circuits that remain shallow at low temperatures. Even when accurate classical state representations are available, compiling them into suitable circuits can be costly. We introduce PP-VarQITE, combining Pauli propagation with variational quantum imaginary-time evolution to determine circuit parameters entirely classically, with target temperatures encoded in the angles of a fixed circuit architecture. In numerical studies of Ising and frustrated Heisenberg models, we recover low-temperature energies and correlations using up to 100 qubits. Across the frustrated chain and two-dimensional lattice, our circuits are approximately two to ten times shallower than the lowest-depth circuits from sequential tensor-network compilation. These shallow circuits enable us to prepare a 48-site frustrated chain and a $7\times7$ Ising lattice using 96 and 98 qubits on the Quantinuum Helios trapped-ion quantum processor. We reproduce the development of dimer order and lattice-wide correlations from high to low temperatures, with the lattice circuits requiring at most 42 two-qubit layers. Through classical circuit design, we demonstrate low-temperature state preparation at circuit sizes beyond the practical reach of statevector simulation.

A machine learning architecture for scaling of holographic quantum circuits

Cliff Sun, Michael L. Wall

We describe an architecture and workflow for learning and generalizing the syntax and parameters of holographic quantum circuits (HQCs), which are natural quantum circuit analogs of classical tensor networks. An HQC uses mid-circuit measurements and resets on a small subset of "physical" qubits whose measurement outcomes become correlated through coupling with a register of persistent "ancilla" qubits, analogous to the physical and bond indices of a tensor network. Hence, an HQC with fixed qubit resources can efficiently generate samples from a large quantum state, and adding qubits to the HQC ansatz has the effect of exponentially increasing the effective bond dimension. Our machine learning approach provides mechanisms for harnessing both features of HQCs by extrapolating the syntax (e.g., a sequence of gates, measurements, and resets) and parameters (e.g., the angles appearing in single-qubit rotations) from a dataset of HQCs to longer or more expressive HQCs. We demonstrate the methodology and its performance on an analytically solvable dataset formed by a vector-valued generalization of the W-state. Finally, we provide an outlook for usage of the methodology for scaling the effective bond dimensions of HQCs in quantum simulation.

Binary-Tree Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm for the Maximum $k$-Cut Problem

Zhaoyang Song, Wei Zhang, Ji Cao, Xuexi Yi, Hong-Fu Wang

The maximum $k$-cut (Max-$k$-Cut) problem, as a natural generalization of the maximum-cut (MaxCut) problem, has broader applications in practical engineering. To solve this problem on quantum devices, existing studies typically encode the $k$ possible subset assignments of each vertex within the quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA), which directly causes the required quantum resources to grow substantially with $k$. Here we propose a binary-tree quantum approximate optimization algorithm (BT-QAOA), which solves the Max-$k$-Cut problem by applying standard MaxCut QAOA to at most $2k-3$ subproblems. Specifically, after solving MaxCut once on the full vertex set, each subsequent step splits only the subset that yields the largest cut-value gain. This process is equivalent to constructing a binary tree whose leaf nodes after $k-1$ steps form an approximate solution to the Max-$k$-Cut problem. Furthermore, we derive a theoretical lower bound on the approximation ratio in terms of the single-step splitting quality, showing that the overall approximation ratio does not decay exponentially across multiple splits. Numerical simulations show that BT-QAOA substantially reduces the single-circuit qubit and gate overhead while maintaining a high approximation ratio, improving the estimated success probability of a single compiled circuit by orders of magnitude. This work effectively expands the practical reach of QAOA on near-term quantum hardware, offering a new route for solving more complex real-world optimization problems with limited quantum resources.

Prospects and limitations of adaptive variational quantum algorithms for lattice field theories: a case study in 2+1 dimensions

Nico Dichter, Andrea Bulgarelli, Lena Funcke, Stefan Kühn

The variational quantum algorithm ADAPT-VQE has previously been used to prepare the vacuum of lattice gauge theories in $1+1D$. In this paper, we study whether it extends to higher dimensions. We identify two general limitations of ADAPT-VQE. First, if the qubit Hamiltonian and the initial state are real, i.e., invariant under qubit-level time-reversal, all real-valued pool operators have vanishing gradients and are never selected. Second, the algorithm can converge to excited eigenstates. We illustrate both limitations, also providing examples from literature, and present a first application of ADAPT-VQE to a lattice field theory in $2+1D$, namely free Wilson fermions, where both obstacles occur. Adding charge conjugation symmetry to the operator pool improves the results, and combining different pools with suitable initial states recovers the ground and first excited states over the full mass range. These findings give practical guidelines for applying ADAPT-VQE to lattice field theories beyond $1+1D$.

Quantum optimization beyond qubit count by low-energy subspace sampling

Mateo Alonso, Gonzalo Blázquez-Gil, Pablo Díez-Valle

On current digital quantum devices, standard one-qubit-per-variable encodings restrict quantum optimization algorithms to problem sizes where classical solvers typically excel. To avoid this constraint, we introduce Quantum Subspace Sampling Descent (QSSD), an iterative heuristic that decouples the number of problem variables from qubit count, while preserving knowledge of the problem's global structure. QSSD drives the energy descent using a fixed-angle Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm as a low-energy sampler. As the angles are precomputed, each iteration requires just a single circuit execution. We test QSSD on IBM Heron and Nighthawk quantum devices with up to 152 qubits on instances of higher-order unconstrained binary optimization and Max-cut problems. On the largest instance with 10000 variables, QSSD reaches an approximation ratio above 0.95 with 100 circuit executions. We provide both exact and heuristic classical baselines, namely greedy, branch-and-bound solver, and simulated annealing. While our approach still does not match the performance of the best classical baseline, it shows promise in a slower runtime scaling with problem size given a fixed budget. This suggests a regime of large problems where subspace sampling on quantum devices could become competitive, as we also demonstrate how improvements in hardware will lead to better algorithm performance. QSSD is thus a practical heuristic for combinatorial optimization problems that helps bring quantum optimization a step closer to utility.

Eitaro Ishikawa, Noah Hirai, Koichiro Miyanishi, Hana Ebi, Kazufumi Tanji, Isamu Kudo

The quantum charge-coupled device (QCCD) architecture is a promising approach to scalable trapped-ion quantum computing, enabling flexible qubit interactions by transporting ions between dedicated trap zones. Programs for such systems require operations beyond conventional circuit primitives: ion shuttling, physical SWAPs, trap splitting and merging, and cooling. Motional excitation accumulated during transport increases the mean phonon number of ions and degrades the fidelity of the two-qubit Molmer-Sorensen gates. However, existing quantum error correction (QEC) simulation frameworks do not jointly track per-ion motional excitation and explicitly schedule cooling operations with their transport time and heating costs. We develop a full-stack software framework for QEC experiments and simulations on linear QCCD systems with a single ion species, comprising an intermediate representation (IR) supporting QCCD-native operations, a compiler that translates syndrome-extraction circuits into the IR, and a heating-aware noise-injection pipeline that generates noisy Stim circuits. The compiler updates the mean phonon number of each ion throughout the schedule, and the pipeline derives gate-dependent error rates from the tracked phonon states and per-ion timing, enabling the evaluation of finite-size logical-error scaling of the rotated surface code under transport- and heating-aware noise. We show that transport-induced heating prevents logical error rates from decreasing with increasing code distance under the evaluated conditions, and that indirectly cooling the ion pairs involved in two-qubit gates, by merging them with precooled coolant ions immediately before each gate, restores this decrease. By sweeping the duration and post-cooling mean phonon number of an abstract cooling instruction, we characterize their trade-off and identify the region in which logical-error scaling is maintained.

Improving Quantum Oblivious Transfer using Zero-Knowledge Proofs

Alex Bredariol Grilo, Lucas Hanouz, Anne Marin

While quantum protocols for oblivious transfer can be built from weaker assumptions than classical ones, the current protocols remain highly impractical due to stringent security bounds that demand a large amount of quantum communication. In this work, we propose an enhancement to quantum oblivious transfer protocols by leveraging zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs). At a high level, we modify the protocol such that the receiver proves that they are following honestly some crucial steps of the protocols, without revealing extra information to the sender. This yields a tighter security bound against a malicious receiver. For practical scenarios with a fixed security target, this approach reduces the amount of required quantum communication by one to two orders of magnitude, depending on the specific parameters. Furthermore, the benefits of our approach become particularly pronounced at higher qubit error rates, and our proposal even extends the maximum tolerable error rate of the quantum channel from approximately 3.5% to about 9%. The ZKP, however, introduces a substantial computational overhead, estimated to significantly exceed the acquisition time of the protocol, which is usually the primary bottleneck. To address this limitation, we also introduce a variant that serves as a tunable trade-off between the protocol with and without our modification. This enables precise adjustment of the computational overhead while still retaining significant, albeit moderately reduced, efficiency gains.

Validity and maximum violation of the entanglement-assisted quantum Singleton bound for linear stabilizer codes

Jae-Hyun Baek, Byung-Sun Won, Jon-Lark Kim

Entanglement-assisted quantum codes protect quantum information with entangled pairs shared in advance, and the entanglement-assisted quantum Singleton bound of Brun, Devetak and Hsieh is the standard estimate of how many pairs they consume. Codes that violate it at large distance are known. For linear entanglement-assisted stabilizer codes, which include all qubit stabilizer codes of this kind, we determine when the bound holds and how far it can fail. The answer depends on how many qudits, together with the receiver's halves of the pairs, recover the logical qudits. If this number is less than twice the number of logical qudits, the bound holds, also for degenerate codes. If it is exactly twice that, the bound overstates the number of pairs by at most the length of the longest classical maximum distance separable (MDS) code of that dimension, minus the dimension. Codes carrying values and derivatives of polynomials on the projective line attain this maximum whenever the field size is at least that dimension and the MDS conjecture is known for it, which includes every prime and, by a recently announced result, every even field size. If the number is more than twice the number of logical qudits, the violation is bounded in terms of the lengths of classical codes of a given Singleton defect, and the bound is attained for one logical qudit.
