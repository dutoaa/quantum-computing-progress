LatentRL-QAS: Transfer via Latent Reinforcement Learning for Quantum Architecture Search

Maria Ilektra Plakitsi, Sebastian Feld, Akash Kundu

Reinforcement learning (RL) emerges as a prominent approach to quantum architecture search (QAS), the automated design of quantum circuits. However, transferring what an agent has learned from one number of qubits to another is still weakly explored: the state, the action space and the neural network of a state-of-the-art RL-QAS agent all grow with the number of qubits, which makes transfer learning across qubit counts prohibitive. We make this transfer possible by introducing LatentRL-QAS, a latent space transfer and refresh framework for RL-based QAS. At its core, a circuit variational autoencoder encodes a circuit as one token per gate and maps circuits on any number of qubits into the same latent space. An RL agent navigates this space instead of adding gates, so its state, actions and network have the same size at every qubit count. The latent space is periodically refreshed with the agent's new discoveries and, for a larger system, grown from the solutions of the smaller one, so what is transferred is the representation of circuits, not network weights or past experience. To show transfer, we consider absolutely maximally entangled (AME) states, a resource for quantum error correction. LatentRL-QAS discovers the five- and six-qubit AME states without being shown them and prepares them in 89-99% of evaluation episodes, whereas the baseline RL-QAS fails to find them with the same budget, even with a network 10 to 17 times larger. LatentRL-QAS remains robust under depolarizing noise with single-qubit gate errors amplified 10x and two-qubit gate errors 5x compared with IBM quantum hardware. By decoupling the RL agent from the number of qubits, LatentRL-QAS lets quantum architecture search reuse circuit representations learned on smaller problems, providing a foundation for the transferable design of circuits across quantum optimization problems.

QPAT: A Universal Quantum Machine Learning Framework for Quantum-Assisted Training and Classical Inference

Yixiong Chen

Deep learning models often struggle with sharp local optima and overfitting in limited-data regimes, and exhibit limited capacity for capturing high-order, complex probability distributions. Quantum Machine Learning (QML) models, by operating in exponentially large Hilbert spaces, can efficiently represent such distributions and provide favorable inductive biases for learning, offering a promising pathway to overcome these limitations. However, their practical deployment in the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era is severely hindered by gradient coupling, barren plateaus, data-loading bottlenecks, and high inference latency. To bridge classical AI and QML, we propose QPAT (QCBM-Prior-Assisted Training), a universal framework that employs a Quantum Circuit Born Machine (QCBM) as a learnable generative prior to provide a task-aligned structured inductive bias during classical model training. The framework can be seamlessly integrated into any classical backbone, including LSTMs, CNNs, and Transformers. We introduce a decoupled fitness-based scoring mechanism and a stochastic "Drop-Prior" mechanism to implicitly distill quantum structural knowledge into the classical weights during training. Evaluated extensively on six benchmarks spanning time-series forecasting, computer vision, and natural language processing, our framework consistently outperforms purely classical backbones in both predictive performance and stability across random seeds; ablation studies further show that these gains are not reproduced by unstructured random priors or a classically learned generative prior. Remarkably, our approach enables completely classical inference, bypassing quantum latency while preserving quantum-enhanced accuracy, paving a highly practical path for QML deployment.

Machine Learning-Based Quantum Error Mitigation for Bound-State Simulations

Sumit Choudhary, Tarunpreet Kaur, Harjeet Kaur, Harpreet Singh

Noise in quantum devices remains a major challenge for reliable quantum simulations, making quantum error mitigation an important tool for improving their accuracy. Machine learning-based quantum error mitigation (ML-QEM) provides a useful approach, but its performance depends on the training datasets and may produce reconstructed quantum states that are not physically valid. In this work, we introduce an ML-QEM framework that uses residual learning with convex projection to ensure that reconstructed density matrices satisfy physically valid density matrices. We study the effect of different training datasets and regression models using Haar-random and variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) ansatz states. We compare the proposed approach with conventional ML-QEM, readout error mitigation, and zero-noise extrapolation. The method is tested on IBM quantum hardware for three- and four-qubit simulations of the bound $Λ$-deuteron and $Λ$-$α$ hypernuclear systems, respectively. For the three-qubit system, ridge regression trained on VQE-ansatz states gives a ground-state energy of -0.134 MeV, close to the ideal value of -0.135 MeV, with a state fidelity of 0.999. For four-qubit system, kernel ridge regression gives ground-state energies of -3.014 and -3.045 MeV, with corresponding state fidelities of 0.995 and 0.998. These results show that physically constrained reconstruction, along with the choice of training datasets and regression models, can improve ML-QEM performance for the quantum simulations studied in this work.

Quantum Algorithm for Direct Estimation of Energy-Level Differences via Quantum Dynamics Simulation

Norifumi Matsumoto, Yuya O. Nakagawa, Shota Kanasugi, Yuichiro Hidaka, Kazunori Maruyama, Hirotaka Oshima

We present a quantum algorithm for the early fault-tolerant quantum computing (early-FTQC) era that estimates minute energy-level differences, such as energy gradients and molecular forces, at a lower sampling cost than existing phase-estimation methods. Such energy derivatives, accessed as the small energy difference induced by an infinitesimal parameter shift, are central to quantum simulation in chemistry and materials science. Although Statistical Phase Estimation (SPE) is well-suited to energy estimation in this era, applying it to an energy difference demands a sampling complexity that scales with the operator norm of the full Hamiltonian, which grows with system size. Our algorithm overcomes this by incorporating two time-evolution operators, corresponding to the Hamiltonians before and after the parameter shift, into a single quantum circuit, which is combined with a non-adaptive, statistically sampled reconstruction in the energy-difference domain. This reduces the relevant scale from the operator norm of the full Hamiltonian to that of the operator norm of the Hamiltonian derivative and the parameter shift, thereby lowering the sampling complexity. We further propose the joint estimation of energy eigenvalues and their differences, giving a holistic view of the energy landscape. Numerical simulations and resource estimations on prototypical systems, such as the Fermi-Hubbard model, demonstrate the resulting reductions in required resources without compromising precision. This positions energy-difference estimation as a practical task for early-FTQC devices, opening a route to molecular forces, response properties, and excited-state energy differences.

Reducing T gates in product formulas with agent loops

Luis Mantilla Calderón, Jérôme F. Gonthier, Alex Zook, Kohei Nakaji, Taylor L. Patti, Alán Aspuru-Guzik

Quantum chemistry calculations on a fault-tolerant quantum computer usually require many $\text{T}$ gates. This cost depends on the simulation method and how precisely its rotations are implemented. In this paper, we used Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, a large language model (LLM), in a coding-agent loop to optimize the structure and rotation-synthesis precision of product formulas (PFs) for quantum phase estimation (QPE). Each of five independent searches optimized a shared protocol across 115 HamLib molecular Hamiltonians spanning 4--16 qubits over 100 code mutations. The searches started from a calibrated, nested second-order PF with uniform synthesis precision. We minimized $T_{95}$, the estimated total $\text{T}$-gate cost including QPE queries and repeated attempts for $95\%$ success probability, while requiring chemical accuracy for every training Hamiltonian. We name the best protocol found error-limited optimized Trotter evolution (ELOTE). It achieves chemical accuracy for its training data and achieved median and geometric-mean $T_{95}$ reductions of $85.35\%$ and $84.82\%$, respectively. ELOTE combines contributions to the same Pauli string, uses a first-order PF, and varies synthesis precision with coefficient magnitude, Pauli support, and diagonal character. We also tested ELOTE on 76 larger Hamiltonians with 18--22 qubits, which met chemical accuracy in every case. For ten reaction energies, we further optimized a shared $δ$-ELOTE protocol. It reduced geometric-mean $T_{95}$ by $58.90\%$ relative to ELOTE while preserving chemical accuracy for every signed reaction difference. These classically verified results show cost savings from shared protocols across molecules and reactions, and motivate similar searches in which energy measurements from quantum computers guide explorations in a coding-agent loop.

A scalable dense silicon qubit architecture with logical operations

Masahiro Tadokoro, Tetsuo Kodera

Fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) requires a large number of physical qubits to encode logical qubits with sufficiently low error rates. Silicon spin qubits are a promising platform because of their small footprint, compatibility with semiconductor manufacturing technologies, and demonstrated high-fidelity operations. However, realizing a scalable architecture that supports quantum error correction, qubit transport, wiring, and logical gate operations remains a major challenge. Here, we propose a scalable dense silicon spin qubit architecture designed to support large-scale FTQC. The architecture consists of qubit memories, data qubit lanes, and cross junctions. Qubit memories store logical qubits encoded using the rotated surface code and support logical single-qubit operations. Data qubit lanes enable high-speed one-dimensional spin shuttling for connectivity between logical qubits, while cross junctions provide routing functionality and implement transversal logical CNOT gates. Using experimentally demonstrated silicon spin qubit parameters, we evaluate logical error rates as a function of code distance and estimate that a code distance of at least 21 is required to achieve logical error rates below $10^{-10}$ per syndrome cycle. We further analyze wiring constraints based on CMOS-compatible multiplexed control schemes and show that, with optimized wiring efficiency, more than 120,000 logical qubits could be integrated on a 100 mm $\times$ 100 mm chip. These results provide a practical roadmap toward large-scale FTQC based on silicon spin qubit technology.

Search-for-Gradient: Search-Based Restarts for Optimisation in Structured Flat Landscapes of Variational Quantum Algorithms

Subham Das, Bikash K. Behera, Andrés Camilo Granda Arango, Giuseppe Sergioli, Roberto Giuntini

Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) are a promising class of hybrid quantum-classical algorithms that involve parameterised quantum circuits and classical optimisation. VQAs have found applications in simulation, optimisation, and machine learning. However, their scalability is hindered by flat landscapes-regions in the parameter space where gradients vanish exponentially small. In such regimes, gradient-based optimisation methods fail because parameter updates become ineffective in the absence of informative gradients. In this work, we propose a search-based restart framework to escape flat regions in structured flat landscapes, in which small trainable regions exist within a large flat landscape. This framework approaches the plateau escape as a search problem and locates trainable regions. The optimiser's parameters stop updating when the gradients vanish, and the method then searches over-sampled points to identify landscapes with nonzero gradients. We provide theoretical estimates of the number of jumps and the probability of escape from flat landscapes. This restarts the local optimisation process and avoids stagnation. We tested this framework on synthetic functions that mimic structured flat landscapes and a real quantum circuit cost landscape. Theoretical analysis and experimental results demonstrate that Search-for-Gradient improves optimisation performance in structured flat landscapes. The proposed framework assumes the existence of a trainable region with nonzero measure and will not work if it has zero measure. Our framework can be integrated with existing VQAs to improve trainability and robustness.

Fewer Parameters, Longer Runtimes: The Wall-Clock Cost of Hybrid Quantum Physics-Informed Neural Networks

Leyan Lyu, Shabnam Jabeen

While hybrid quantum-classical physics-informed neural networks (QCPINNs) are often described as efficient because they have fewer trainable parameters than classical PINNs, parameter count does not measure wall-clock cost. To examine this gap on a one-dimensional seawater temperature diffusion benchmark, with the quantum circuits simulated on a CPU, we time the four cumulative stages a PINN needs (the forward pass, the first- and second-order input gradients, and the PDE-loss parameter gradient) for a classical PINN, four hybrid configurations that differ in qubit count and circuit depth, and size-matched classical controls that share the hybrids' classical layers. We find that the hybrids are 14-56x slower than the classical PINN for the forward pass and 10-97x slower for the PDE-loss parameter gradient, and even against the size-matched controls, they remain 9-85x slower. Further, over the tested ranges, runtime increased approximately exponentially with qubit count, approximately linearly with circuit depth, and sub-linearly with batch size, while for the circuit-only forward pass, a second simulator backend was 10-165x slower under the same calling pattern. Although the hybrids have 38-44% fewer parameters than the classical PINN, this reduction comes from their smaller classical layers, as the quantum circuit accounts for at most 2.5% of each hybrid's parameters. Yet parameter count failed to predict runtime even among the classical networks, since the size-matched controls, despite having fewer parameters than the classical PINN, run slower than it. Our conclusion is that fewer parameters did not mean lower cost: every hybrid took at least ten times longer than the classical PINN at every stage, and the gap grew quickly with the number of qubits.

In the long term, fault tolerance bridges this gap, but simply changing the numerical base from binary to higher base d is a viable way to 'squeeze more juice'---get more quantum information processing---from a quantum computer of given size. Here, we review recent experimental progress in high-fidelity preparation, control, and measurement of qudits across various platforms such as trapped ions, superconducting circuits, solid-state nuclear spins, photonic systems, and neutral atoms. We highlight early demonstrations of qudit-based quantum simulation, computation and error suppression enabled by these developments, heralding the transition of qudits from conceptual constructs to viable building blocks for quantum technologies. Finally, we outline key challenges and potential future directions for scalable qudit quantum information processing.

Thermal state preparation with shallow quantum circuits

Cenk Tüysüz, Simon Deconihout, Enrico Rinaldi, Michele Grossi

Preparing thermal states on noisy quantum hardware requires circuits that remain shallow at low temperatures. Even when accurate classical state representations are available, compiling them into suitable circuits can be costly. We introduce PP-VarQITE, combining Pauli propagation with variational quantum imaginary-time evolution to determine circuit parameters entirely classically, with target temperatures encoded in the angles of a fixed circuit architecture. In numerical studies of Ising and frustrated Heisenberg models, we recover low-temperature energies and correlations using up to 100 qubits. Across the frustrated chain and two-dimensional lattice, our circuits are approximately two to ten times shallower than the lowest-depth circuits from sequential tensor-network compilation. These shallow circuits enable us to prepare a 48-site frustrated chain and a $7\times7$ Ising lattice using 96 and 98 qubits on the Quantinuum Helios trapped-ion quantum processor. We reproduce the development of dimer order and lattice-wide correlations from high to low temperatures, with the lattice circuits requiring at most 42 two-qubit layers. Through classical circuit design, we demonstrate low-temperature state preparation at circuit sizes beyond the practical reach of statevector simulation.

The integrable Lie-Trotter-Suzuki decomposition for nonlinear dynamics: Efficient Hamiltonian simulation of the Stepanoff flow

Ilon Joseph, Ivan Novikau, Michael R. Montgomery, Joanna Slawinska, Dimitrios Giannakis

We propose an efficient unitary numerical time integration strategy for nonlinear dynamical systems: the integrable Lie-Trotter-Suzuki decomposition (I-LTS). First, express the generator as a sum of integrable parts. Second, approximate the full evolution operator using a Lie-Trotter-Suzuki (LTS) decomposition of the desired approximation order in time step. The I-LTS construction ensures that the full evolution operator can be represented as a product of terms that each have an efficient explicit tensor product factorization that can be handled efficiently with tensor network and/or quantum block-encoding methods. Next, we apply this method to an important non-integrable point example of a dynamical system: the Stepanoff flow on the torus. First, we derive an efficient unitary quantum algorithm for simulating the Stepanoff flow that can be thought of as a unitary classical version of a quantum map. Then, we provide a detailed description of the encoding of the two-dimensional system into an efficient quantum circuit, including an efficient block-encoding for all required operators. An efficient Hamiltonian simulation algorithm is constructed using a sequence of alternating quantum Fourier transforms (QFT) and Quantum Signal Processing (QSP) circuits. Finally, the circuit is simulated using a numerical emulator of fault-tolerant quantum computers, and the results are found to be consistent with several alternative classical computational methods. While efficient, the need for Trotterization introduces fundamental complexity in requiring small enough time step for an accurate approximation and solving for the transfer operator requires one to confront the linear scaling of the advection operator with grid size.

A machine learning architecture for scaling of holographic quantum circuits

Cliff Sun, Michael L. Wall

We describe an architecture and workflow for learning and generalizing the syntax and parameters of holographic quantum circuits (HQCs), which are natural quantum circuit analogs of classical tensor networks. An HQC uses mid-circuit measurements and resets on a small subset of "physical" qubits whose measurement outcomes become correlated through coupling with a register of persistent "ancilla" qubits, analogous to the physical and bond indices of a tensor network. Hence, an HQC with fixed qubit resources can efficiently generate samples from a large quantum state, and adding qubits to the HQC ansatz has the effect of exponentially increasing the effective bond dimension. Our machine learning approach provides mechanisms for harnessing both features of HQCs by extrapolating the syntax (e.g., a sequence of gates, measurements, and resets) and parameters (e.g., the angles appearing in single-qubit rotations) from a dataset of HQCs to longer or more expressive HQCs. We demonstrate the methodology and its performance on an analytically solvable dataset formed by a vector-valued generalization of the W-state. Finally, we provide an outlook for usage of the methodology for scaling the effective bond dimensions of HQCs in quantum simulation.

Neural Network Decoders For Qubit Loss

J. Wilson Staples, Winston Fu, Yihe Dong, Jeff D. Thompson

Qubit loss is a significant source of error in many quantum hardware platforms, requiring circuits that periodically replace or reset qubits to maintain fault-tolerance. When the reset process includes loss-resolving measurements, this information can improve decoding and substantially reduce overhead compared to the case where this information is not available. Prior work has demonstrated loss-tolerant circuits for the surface code that show significantly improved thresholds and subthreshold scaling by incorporating loss information. However, the computational complexity of loss simulation and decoding, which falls outside of the usual stabilizer framework, has limited extending these studies to high-rate qLDPC codes, which are already challenging to decode in the absence of loss. In this work, we present a loss-aware convolutional neural-network (CNN) decoder framework that circumvents this challenge. Training is enabled by a novel sampler, \texttt{slim}, which achieves a throughput for circuits with Pauli noise and loss that is comparable to the state-of-the-art for pure Pauli noise. On the surface code with loss, the decoder matches the accuracy of computationally expensive most-likely error (MLE) decoders at much lower latency. For the $[[144,12,12]]$ bivariate bicycle (Gross) code, we study a walking syndrome extraction circuit with Pauli and loss errors. For physical error rates $p<1\%$, we observe a more than $10^3$-fold lower logical error rate for a pure loss model, relative to a pure Pauli error model at the same physical error rate, along with significantly enhanced sub-threshold error rate scaling. This demonstrates that loss-dominated error models can also significantly improve performance in a high-rate qLDPC code, and provides a simulation and decoder training platform that can be extended to other codes and algorithmic gadgets.
