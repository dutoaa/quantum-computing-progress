Agentic Pipelines for Mapping Topological Codes to Quantum Hardware

Arpit Arora, Adam Holmes, Krysta M. Svore, Taylor L. Patti

Mapping a prescribed many-body Hamiltonian to a particular quantum hardware is generally an approximate process, resulting in both intended and unwanted quantum dynamics. We introduce an AI-agent-assisted workflow that uses Lean to establish whether an effective device Hamiltonian retains the intended low-energy subspace of the target many-body Hamiltonian. We demonstrate this approach with topological quantum error correction (QEC), providing, to our knowledge, the first Lean-verified pipeline connecting a code space to low-energy states of a device model. Starting from qubits and parity checks, the pipeline constructs the ideal Hamiltonian whose lowest energy states form the intended code space, and determines the excitation gap that protects that code space. It then tests if the device retains the required number of isolated low-energy states and quantifies how closely their subspace matches the intended code space. We compare three bounds on unwanted energy shifts obtained by: summing the interaction strengths, grouping the anticommuting Pauli interactions, and diagonalizing the Hamiltonian. Pauli grouping yields a bound that is tighter than the conventional sum and closer to the diagonalization result, avoiding overly conservative compensation requirements. The workflow also identifies the strongest unwanted interactions and the qubits they affect, providing specific targets for improving device couplings and controls. We demonstrate our framework using an eight-qubit toric-code array on a superconducting device, where the same driven device generates both the required four-qubit interactions as well as unwanted dynamics. An AI agent identifies viable device parameters within a prescribed leakage budget, and Lean verifies four isolated low-energy states close to the ideal code space. Our workflow supports predictive hardware engineering for analog implementations of QEC codes.

QYANTRAM addresses this gap through an open platform comprising four subsystems, built around evidence-backed results and independent verification. Reimagining computational quantum research with AI scientists

Yuxuan Du, Hanqing Zhao, Xinbiao Wang, Yiming Lu, Kaining Zhang, Dacheng Tao

AI scientists are expanding automated research across scientific domains, yet their ability to turn the scientific record into a foundation for autonomous discovery remains elusive. Here we uncover a fundamental barrier to this transition and demonstrate a route to overcome it in computational quantum science, where advances shape both what we can learn from complex quantum systems and what quantum computing can ultimately deliver. To identify this barrier, we introduce OmniQ, an expert-audited virtual research environment that converts published studies into executable reproduction tasks. Across 59 papers spanning classical simulation, quantum algorithms and quantum-property characterization, we systematically evaluate five agentic systems powered by large language models and six third-party agent harnesses. Our findings reveal that current AI scientists often fail to reproduce published results, exposing a fundamental mismatch between what scientific papers communicate and what autonomous agents require. By contrast, human expert intervention can turn these failures into successful reproductions, showing that the missing information can be identified and converted into reusable research experience. Guided by this observation, we develop Residual Agents (RA), a quantum-tailored self-improving AI scientist that distils externally verified workflow repairs into such experience. RA substantially improves research performance under fixed base models and limited adaptation budgets. For example, RA raises DeepSeek V4.1 Flash performance from 68% to 85%, matching Claude Code with Opus 5 at a third of its cost despite using a weaker base model. We integrate all reproduced results into an extensible platform built on the model context protocol, making verified research workflows directly accessible to AI scientists and allowing new scientific papers to be continuously incorporated.

Iterative Quantum Optimization: From Greedy Reductions to Branch-and-Bound

Stuart Hadfield, Lucas T. Braydwood, Eleanor Rieffel

Iterative quantum optimization (IQO) uses quantum-generated information to select a sequence of classical problem reductions. We introduce Bound-Aware Tree-IQO which combines this iterative structure with bounds and reversible search. Feasible measured samples update a shared incumbent, while valid classical bounds prune branches that cannot improve it. Incorporating backtracking yields an anytime method that returns the best feasible solution found when interrupted, or can be run as a complete search to return and certify the optimum. For suitable single-variable or pairwise reductions, an initial IQO path can determine the branching schedule without further quantum calls, though certifying the optimum may take superpolynomial classical time. To motivate our framework, we prove two limitations of irreversible IQO reductions based on the quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA). We show an infinite family of unweighted MaxCut instances on which globally optimized level-1 QAOA makes standard Recursive QAOA strictly prefer a first reduction that excludes every optimal solution, and limits that branch to an approximation ratio of at most $2/3+o(1)$. In contrast, a single backtrack recovers an optimal solution using the same quantum information. Causal cone arguments give a complementary obstruction at every fixed depth of standard QAOA: a weighted MaxCut instance is shown to contain two edges with identical two-qubit reduced states, even though every optimum requires opposite edge parities. MaxCut experiments show that QAOA-guided ordering is nonrandom in exact-certification tests through $n=28$, while demonstrating that quantum samples can strengthen the incumbent and enable further pruning. Our results suggest that quantum data could provide useful guidance toward solving hard combinatorial optimization problems more effectively than either quantum or classical algorithms alone.

eigenkit: A Provider-Neutral Quantum Computing Library

Laia Domingo, Daniil Tarpanov, Arsenii Senokosov, Asel Sagingalieva

Quantum software ecosystems provide increasingly capable tools for simulation and hardware execution, but differences in programming models, supported features, and backend capabilities can make it difficult to move and systematically compare the same quantum algorithm across execution environments. We introduce eigenkit, a provider-neutral quantum computing library that separates algorithm definition from backend execution through a common circuit representation and provider interface. The library currently supports 12 execution providers spanning simulators and quantum hardware, provides backend-independent gradient computation, and integrates with standard scientific-computing and machine-learning workflows through NumPy, PyTorch, and scikit-learn interfaces. We evaluate the practical relevance of this abstraction across both simulation and quantum hardware. Simulator performance varies substantially with circuit size, structure, batching, and application workload, showing that no single backend is consistently preferable. On quantum hardware, experiments on IBM Heron and IQM Emerald show that which processor is better suited to a given algorithm depends not only on hardware constraints, but also on how well the topology of the quantum algorithm matches the physical connectivity of the processor. These results demonstrate that backend portability is not only a software-design convenience, but can be important for both computational efficiency and quantum execution quality.

MBUndo: Sound, Automatic, and Efficient Measurement-Based Uncomputation for Quantum Circuits

Shengyuan Yang, Charles Yuan

Quantum programs that use temporary values, known as \emph{ancilla} qubits, must explicitly \emph{uncompute} each ancilla back to zero to ensure that the program produces correct outputs. Conventional uncomputation uses unitary quantum gates, whereas leading quantum algorithms now leverage \emph{measurement-based uncomputation} (MBU), which replaces costly unitary $T$ gates with cheaper measurements and phase corrections. Current quantum programming frameworks can use measurement to uncompute specific gates such as Toffoli. No automatic transformation, however, accepts an arbitrary circuit whose ancillas are computed and uncomputed via the standard approach and converts the circuit to use MBU\@. Researchers instead apply MBU to individual circuit designs by hand, which limits algorithm development and compiler optimization. Such a transformation has not existed due to two fundamental unknowns: where MBU applies and which gates realize MBU for an arbitrary computation. To solve these, we first present a static analysis that identifies, for each ancilla qubit, each circuit position where measurement is sound --- subsuming basic assumptions that MBU only applies at the end of a circuit. Next, we present circuit transformations that soundly turn unitary uncomputation gates into efficient phase corrections after measurement --- generalizing rewrite rules from prior literature that are either limited to a single gate or not applicable to a general circuit. We present \textsf{MBUndo}, an integrated compiler pass that automatically converts circuits to measurement-based uncomputation. It requires no user annotations other than which qubits are ancillas, the same as needed by existing uncomputation synthesizers, and can enhance their output circuits. We evaluate \textsf{MBUndo} on 22 benchmarks to reveal \emph{T} gate reductions of ${>}36.5\%$ over uncomputation synthesizers.

Quantum channel tomography: optimal bounds in the near-boundary regime

Kean Chen, Aadil Oufkir

Quantum channel tomography (also known as quantum process tomography) is a fundamental task in quantum information and quantum computing, aiming to learn the full description of an unknown quantum channel from black-box queries. For channels with input dimension $d_1$, output dimension $d_2$, and Kraus rank at most $r$, [CGO+26] identifies the dilation rate $τ=rd_2/d_1\geq 1$ as a key parameter governing a Heisenberg-to-classical transition in query complexity. While the optimal bounds in the boundary regime ($τ=1$) and the away-from-boundary regime ($τ\geq 1+Ω(1)$) are now known, the precise behavior in the near-boundary regime ($1<τ<1+o(1)$) has remained unresolved. In this paper, we close this gap, thereby fully settling the query complexity of quantum channel tomography. Specifically, we show that the query complexity is - $Ω(rd_1d_2/\varepsilon^2)$ for diamond norm error $\varepsilon$, matching the upper bound by [MB26, CGO+26], - $Θ(d_1^2/\varepsilon+\min\{(τ-1)d_1^2,(τ-1)^2d_1^2d_2^2\}/\varepsilon^2)$ for Choi trace norm error $\varepsilon$. Our results reveal two qualitatively different forms of the Heisenberg-to-classical transition. For diamond norm tomography, the Heisenberg scaling disappears suddenly as soon as $τ$ leaves the boundary regime. In contrast, Choi trace norm tomography exhibits a gradual transition from Heisenberg to classical scaling as $τ$ moves from the boundary regime to the away-from-boundary regime.

Leaving Quantum Bias on the Table: Pushforwards as a Path to Quantum Advantage in Constrained Optimization

Chinonso Onah, Stuart Hadfield, Kristel Michielsen

Constrained quantum samplers often produce infeasible outputs that retain information about good feasible solutions. We develop a post-measurement framework that constructs problem-dependent classical feasibility maps from constraint-violation statistics and pushes the raw quantum sampling law forward onto the feasible set. The probability of each repaired solution is the total raw mass of its repair preimage, enlarging the successful sampling event from a target feasible set to its full repair preimage. We derive exact identities for repaired success and expected cost, and separate repair-basin geometry from sampler-dependent probability concentration. For noisy implementations, we distinguish leakage from the encoded subspace from concentration loss within it. For permutation constraints, Hungarian maximum-overlap repair provides an O(n^3) implementation, which we study on traveling-salesman problems (TSP). We derive conditional repaired-success bounds and quantify how basin aggregation reduces the absolute-transfer budget required by these bounds. Noiseless simulations show that optimal repair basins contain selectively overweighted infeasible contributors. For an n=10 TSP instance, pushforward repair recovered the optimum using approximately 10^3 shots, compared with 10^6 shots in the original CE-QAOA workflow without repair. On a 127-qubit IBM processor, using encodings of up to 100 qubits, repaired candidates match reference tours on smaller benchmarks and improve them on larger instances. These results establish concentration of quantum probability on useful repair preimages as a design principle for constrained quantum optimization.

Scaling Laws for Parameter Setting in Digitized Counterdiabatic QAOA

Yaocheng Chen, Jiapei Zhuang

Optimizing parameters separately for each problem instance limits the reuse of variational quantum algorithms. We develop empirical scaling laws for parameter setting in the digitized counterdiabatic quantum approximate optimization algorithm (DC-QAOA) for weighted MaxCut. The prescription combines exact covariance under uniform edge-weight rescaling with power laws in mean degree fitted separately to offline-optimized cost and counterdiabatic angles. After the weight and degree scales are removed, the optimized angles of different training graphs cluster around common amplitudes, so a small coefficient table suffices. Frozen coefficients assign angles from a target graph's mean degree and root-mean-square edge weight without target-instance variational optimization. In ideal-circuit simulations across 1050 test graphs spanning ten graph families and five positive edge-weight distributions, mean optimal-solution probability is higher than that of the fixed-angle quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA) at each depth from one to four layers, reaching 19.36% versus 13.11% at four layers. A six-layer extension on 600 graphs also shows a mean probability advantage. Hardware execution on the IBM Quantum processor ibm_fez for 21 unit-weight 3-regular graphs shows a mean probability advantage at one layer, while the ordering reverses at two layers. In separate energy-based experiments, direct assignment gives a higher mean approximation ratio than random-start optimization reaches within 500 evaluations, and refinement started from the formula angles improves it further. These results show how a scaling-based representation of optimized angles can guide parameter reuse across graph instances, enabling direct parameter assignment without target-instance variational optimization and providing initial angles for local refinement.

Quantum simulation of dissipative acoustic and elastic waves by Schrödingerisation: absorbing boundaries and explicit polynomial-size circuits

Hoang Anh Nguyen, Ali Tura

Explicit quantum circuits for the acoustic and elastic wave equations, obtained by Hamiltonian simulation of their first-order forms, conserve energy and therefore describe closed domains: an outgoing wave reflects off the edge of the computational region or, with periodic boundaries, wraps around and re-enters from the opposite side. We open the domain with the simplest physical absorber, a graded sponge layer in which every wave field is damped at a rate that grows towards the outer edge, and implement the resulting dissipative dynamics on a quantum simulator by Schrödingerisation, which turns a non-unitary linear evolution into a family of unitary ones by means of a single auxiliary variable. We derive the whole chain from the partial differential equation to the circuit, in one, two and three dimensions. An independent finite-difference solver confirms that damping all wave fields absorbs far better than the common velocity-only damping mask. For the sponge the quantum analysis is sharp: recovery is exact on the whole non- negative half of the auxiliary axis at every time; that axis can be truncated periodically at a length set by the largest damping rate and the simulated time; and the success probability is a fixed constant, about 0.4, times the fraction of the energy not yet absorbed, with only one or two auxiliary qubits measured. On the circuit side, the damping step, which generic synthesis compiles into a number of controlled-NOT gates exponential in the number of qubits, factorises exactly into polynomially many multi-controlled phase gates. Gate-level simulations on up to 25 qubits agree with the classical solutions of the damped wave equations.

On the same circuits, a Pauli-measurement VQE deviates by 791-1,129 bp, so the single-setting estimator gives about ten times smaller errors with one setting instead of nine. Spin SYK-dot chains: entanglement bound and resource estimates towards a quantum advantage

Sayantan Dey, Abel Carreras, Siddhartha Patra

Ground states of Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK)-type models are volume-law entangled, which makes them costly both for tensor networks and for quantum hardware simulations. Thus, it remains unclear where quantum hardware could outperform classical simulation of such models. In this work, we study a spin SYK-dot chain, in which q dense SYK dots of p qubits are joined by single ZZ bonds of strength K. The entanglement across any cut is then bounded by floor(p/2) ln 2 plus the inter-dot entanglement, and the two-qubit depth of one Trotter step is set by p alone. A resource estimate shows that a quantum advantage for the ground state could appear only for dots of about 15 to 21 qubits or more. There, a single Trotter step already requires two-qubit error rates of order 10^{-6}, beyond the reach of noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware. We test the assumptions of this estimate with an imaginary-time matrix product state (MPS) that applies each dot as one block. It agrees with exact diagonalisation at (p, q) = (6, 2). At (5, 2), a variational circuit with the same dot-plus-link layout comes within 3.6% of the exact energy, and noisy simulations show that its fidelity decays as assumed in the resource estimate. At (8, 5), i.e., N = 40 qubits, the energy converges at bond dimension chi = 768, far below the exact value 2^20, and the inter-dot entanglement stays small and is set mainly by K. Out-of-time-order correlators across the links decay on a time scale that shrinks rapidly with |K|. Thus, the dot size p and the inter-dot coupling K together set the entanglement of the chain, and with it where a quantum advantage could appear.
