Band structure of two-dimensional graphene and of one-dimensional nanoribbons via variational quantum algorithms: from energies to wavefunction quality

Raúl Medrano Millán, Rodrigo Gil-Merino y Rubio, Juan Borge, Unai Aseguinolaza Aguirreche

Simulating quantum many-body systems classically encounters exponential bottlenecks, which quantum devices circumvent by natively encoding wavefunctions. In the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era, however, hardware noise constrains simulation accuracy. As a stepping stone toward strongly correlated materials, we compute the band structure of 2D monolayer graphene and 1D nanoribbons (zigzag and armchair) using a single variational workflow in the $p_z$ tight-binding approximation. Non-interacting ground states also provide essential initial reference states for subspace and warm-started variational methods to solve interacting spectra. By mapping the Bloch Hamiltonian to Pauli operators, we extract the ground band via the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and excited subbands via Variational Quantum Deflation (VQD), validating our approach from ideal simulations to execution on physical IBM Quantum processors. In ideal simulations, the workflow reproduces exact diagonalization results with high precision, whereas hardware noise introduces energy deviations of a few hundredths of the hopping parameter. While qubit counts scale logarithmically with ribbon width, fixed-depth ansatz accuracy degrades with Hilbert space dimension. Crucially, deepening the ansatz improves ideal simulation accuracy by over three orders of magnitude but worsens energy estimates under noise, proving that practical circuit depth is bounded by hardware noise rather than expressivity. Finally, we complement energy evaluations with a wavefunction-level diagnostic measuring subspace containment. This diagnostic captures degenerate manifolds and exposes optimization failure modes disguised by energy agreement alone. Operating directly on NISQ hardware, this validated workflow establishes a controlled baseline for studying topological and strongly correlated phases in realistic materials.

Runtime Compilation for Quantum Circuits with PQF-Enabled $R_z$ Synthesis

Siddharth Dangwal, Christopher Kang, Jad Habhab, Ritvik Sharma, Frederic T. Chong, Gokul Subramanian Ravi

In Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computing (FTQC) using surface codes, reducing $T$-count is critical for efficient computation due to the high cost of $T$-state distillation. Probabilistic Quantum Circuit with Fallback (PQF) synthesis significantly lowers $T$-counts compared to standard protocols like Gridsynth, but introduces high circuit dynamism through extra gates inserted at runtime. This dynamism invalidates static compile-time routing, as these new $T$ gates require dynamic lattice surgery routing to establish non-intersecting ancilla paths to $T$-state factories while minimizing total execution time. To address this, we introduce $\text{R}^2$-PQF, a Runtime Routing protocol for PQF-synthesized quantum circuits. $\text{R}^2$-PQF dynamically routes gates layer-by-layer, computing paths during the execution of the previous logical timestep. To satisfy the strict computational window of $\sim t_{lattice\_surgery} + t_{reaction}$ and non-intersectionality constraints, we implement a Wavefront Breadth-First Search (W-BFS) and synthesise it on a 500MHz FPGA and 2 GHz ASIC. For a clock cycle of 2GHz, we achieve a median logical time-step of $1.51 μs$ for a 300 qubit Ising Hamiltonian circuit, demonstrating that $\text{R}^2$-PQF successfully resolves the vast majority of paths (with rare exceptions) within the execution window and enables the $T$-count benefits of PQF without latency penalties. Compared to a baseline stalling-based solution (the only no-runtime-recomputation solution), $\text{R}^2$-PQF speeds up circuit execution by $1.34\times$ on average and up to $1.76 \times$.

On Revolutionizing Power Systems: Suitability of Quantum Computing-Ready Pipelines for Stochastic Unit Commitment Optimization

Ying-Yi Hong, Dylan Josh Domingo Lopez

Modern unit commitment (UC) scenarios, particularly those involving uncertainties from demand fluctuations and renewable energy sources, pose significant challenges to traditional optimization methods due to the large search spaces they encompass. This paper proposes a quantum computing-ready optimization pipeline for deterministic and stochastic UC, integrating classical, quantum-inspired, quantum annealing, and hybrid quantum-classical solvers across test systems, including the IEEE 118-bus system. A novel Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) reformulation accommodates generator scaling and 24-hour scheduling horizons with compact Boolean logic penalties for startup, cooldown, and spinning reserve constraints. A divide-and-conquer strategy and Quantum Computing Monte Carlo (QCMC) simulation address scalability across 27,730 uncertainty scenarios, reducing compilation time by up to two orders of magnitude. Experimental results demonstrate the pipeline's ability to achieve efficient, scalable, and accurate UC optimization under uncertainty: the hybrid quantum method approach matches the classical baseline, reduces stochastic UC runtime from 40.76 to 14.79 hours, and characterizes pure quantum annealing as limited to small systems with 0.22% to 2.18% encoding errors and constraint violations. These results provide an analysis of the scalability and computational practicality of hybrid quantum optimization for generator scheduling in UC.

Hybrid Quantum--Classical Pauli-Correlator Architecture for Image Reconstruction

Sparsho Chakraborty, Siddhartha Patra

We introduce the Pauli-Correlator Autoencoder (PCAE), a hybrid quantum--classical architecture for image reconstruction. PCAE splits an image into patches and compresses each patch as a matrix product state (MPS). A small quantum circuit turns each compressed patch into a few measured numbers, and a classical neural network rebuilds the patch from these numbers and the patch position. A learnable energy term, taken from physics, is added to training and tracked as training proceeds. Two versions place the six qubits on a line (PCAE1D) or on a grid (PCAE2D). An averaging step makes the PCAE2D features follow rotations and reflections of the image exactly. Trained on one image at a time, PCAE2D rebuilds images from six public datasets with a peak signal-to-noise ratio of 25.8 to 41.6 decibels. On CIFAR-10 images not used in training, a formula-based stand-in for the PCAE2D features performs within 1 decibel of several simpler classical methods. Five trained models also run on real IBM quantum computers without retraining, and rebuild images at 25.90 to 31.52 decibels. A short argument and a synthetic test show that, with a linear final layer, measuring qubits in pairs is needed for targets built from products of inputs. These results show that PCAE works as a hybrid model that rebuilds varied images and runs on today's quantum hardware at the six-qubit scale.

The results show that this framework can produce competitive solutions, supporting its use as a flexible mapping strategy for continuous-variable optimization in qubit-based quantum and quantum-inspired pipelines.

Conditional Quantum Priors for Chaotic Fluid Dynamics

Shao-Bo Zhou, Yu-Ze Zhu, Hang Song, Xiang Zhao, Lin Yang, Xian-Min Jin

Neural networks that predict chaotic dynamics can reproduce short trajectories while misrepresenting statistics over long times. Quantum priors represent selected dynamical statistics through quantum generative models and provide statistical constraints for classical neural networks. Training a separate prior for each value of a system parameter, such as the Reynolds number, does not explicitly model how these statistics vary with that parameter. We learn a conditional quantum prior that approximates selected statistics of an invariant measure at unseen parameter values within the training range, without retraining. For Lorenz-63, the prior reduces mean Jensen-Shannon divergence by 56.9% relative to a classical multilayer perceptron with a comparable number of weights across 96 unseen parameter values. Across 101 values of the same parameter, the shared prior uses 84.7% fewer trainable weights than would be required by a separate quantum neural network at each value. We then use the learned prior to regularize classical neural networks, encouraging their autoregressive rollouts to better reproduce long-term statistics. For Kolmogorov flow at Reynolds number $Re=1000$, prior regularization reduces forecast mean squared error by 66.0% over 318 steps relative to the classical baseline without prior regularization. We also implement a photonic quantum prior for noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices, achieving a mean Jensen-Shannon divergence of $4.58\times10^{-3}$ for Lorenz-63 and $2.59\times10^{-3}$ for Kolmogorov flow at unseen parameter values.

Agentic Pipelines for Mapping Topological Codes to Quantum Hardware

Arpit Arora, Adam Holmes, Krysta M. Svore, Taylor L. Patti

Mapping a prescribed many-body Hamiltonian to a particular quantum hardware is generally an approximate process, resulting in both intended and unwanted quantum dynamics. We introduce an AI-agent-assisted workflow that uses Lean to establish whether an effective device Hamiltonian retains the intended low-energy subspace of the target many-body Hamiltonian. We demonstrate this approach with topological quantum error correction (QEC), providing, to our knowledge, the first Lean-verified pipeline connecting a code space to low-energy states of a device model. Starting from qubits and parity checks, the pipeline constructs the ideal Hamiltonian whose lowest energy states form the intended code space, and determines the excitation gap that protects that code space. It then tests if the device retains the required number of isolated low-energy states and quantifies how closely their subspace matches the intended code space. We compare three bounds on unwanted energy shifts obtained by: summing the interaction strengths, grouping the anticommuting Pauli interactions, and diagonalizing the Hamiltonian. Pauli grouping yields a bound that is tighter than the conventional sum and closer to the diagonalization result, avoiding overly conservative compensation requirements. The workflow also identifies the strongest unwanted interactions and the qubits they affect, providing specific targets for improving device couplings and controls. We demonstrate our framework using an eight-qubit toric-code array on a superconducting device, where the same driven device generates both the required four-qubit interactions as well as unwanted dynamics. An AI agent identifies viable device parameters within a prescribed leakage budget, and Lean verifies four isolated low-energy states close to the ideal code space. Our workflow supports predictive hardware engineering for analog implementations of QEC codes.

DynaMite: Dynamic Measurement Scheduling To Accelerate Fault-Tolerant Quantum Programs

Dongwhee Kim, Suhas Vittal, Poulami Das

Fault-tolerant quantum computers run millions of logical cycles to perform quantum error correction (QEC). As logical operations span entire logical cycles, the logical cycle time (LCT) dictates program runtime. Qubit measurements, critical to extract parity information for QEC, are the slowest operations and account for 43-86% of LCT. Measurement latencies impact fidelity: fast measurements lower our ability to correctly classify quantum states, whereas slow measurements increase the likelihood of decoherence. Hardware providers thus calibrate measurement latencies that maximize readout fidelity per qubit and use them to schedule all measurements during program execution. However, calibrated latencies are sub-optimal for logical performance because logical cycles span multiple QEC rounds, during which data qubits are not measured. This increases LCT and logical error rates (LERs) due to decoherence at the logical level. We propose DynaMite, a lightweight real-time measurement latency selection policy built on two key insights. First, logical cycles must use a measurement latency that is fast enough to reduce both LCT and LER for the QEC code, instead of the calibrated latency that only maximizes readout fidelity. Second, not all logical cycles are equal; many do not fully utilize the error budget of the QEC code, i.e., the number of failed parity checks it can tolerate without compromising LER. These cycles could use even faster measurements but do not, because latencies are chosen statically, whereas error information is only available at runtime. DynaMite exploits the fact that a logical cycle comprises multiple QEC rounds and selects the fastest latency for an upcoming round based on the error budget left and rounds remaining. Our evaluations show that DynaMite reduces LCT by 1.69x on average and up to 2.29x, and reduces LER to 0.46x on average and up to 0.13x of baseline.

Data informed Ansatz Selection for Variational Quantum Circuits

Manav Seksaria, Advith Desu, Anil Prabhakar

Choosing a variational quantum circuit (VQC) usually means searching over ansatze and depths, and training each candidate. We give three rules that fix these choices from the data and the gate set alone, before any training. First, a circuit without entangling gates has a rank-one Fourier coefficient tensor on every subset of qubits. A singular value decomposition of a dataset's Fourier coefficients therefore indicates whether entanglement is needed, which qubits it connects, and a lower bound on the error of omitting it. Second, the rank of the quantum Fisher information matrix grows by $p_0$ per layer, after a first-layer deficit $d_0$, until it reaches a ceiling set by the dynamical Lie algebra. This law fixes the depth. Third, reading out all $2^N - 1$ Pauli-Z products from one batch of shots resolves half of the state's directions, the most any single-basis readout can. A single-qubit readout resolves an exponentially small fraction. For the Madelon dataset, the rules prescribe 7 blocks of 3 qubits at depth 3: a 239-parameter classifier with 78.5% test accuracy, found without a grid search. Ablations confirm three predicted effects: shallower circuits, smaller blocks and dropping reuploading all lower accuracy.

Quantum circuit optimization is an indispensable component of the quantum software stack, used to reduce noise, maximize performance, alleviate costs of quantum error correction, or target specific hardware. A related problem is approximate membership: for a fixed set of unitaries $S$, decide whether a given circuit $C$ is within distance $\varepsilon$ of a unitary in $S$ under some metric $m$. Membership is a special case of optimization, where $S$ is a set of unitaries implementable at no cost (e.g., with noise-minimal circuits, or without magic states). Janzing, Wocjan, and Beth (Int. J. Quantum Inf., 2005) showed that, for the operator metric, the case $S=\{I\}$ (approximate identity) is QMA-complete when $C$ is promised to be either close to the identity or far from it. We generalize this setting in three directions: (i) promise-free inputs, (ii) besides $S=\{I\}$, sets $S$ containing only Clifford unitaries, or only generalized permutation matrices (i.e., no superposition), and (iii) the trace-based, spectral, and operator metrics. We show that, in all cases, approximate membership is PP-hard for wide ranges of $\varepsilon$, provided $C$'s gate-set can implement Clifford+T, as the gate-sets of standard hardware do. If $S=\{I\}$, or if $S$ contains only generalized permutation matrices, $I\in S$, and $m$ is a trace-based distance, PP-hardness holds for all non-trivial $\varepsilon$. If $S=\{I\}$ and $m$ is a trace-based distance, PP-hardness holds even for circuits over classical-reversible gate-sets. Our results indicate that approximate membership is harder over promise-free inputs than the QMA-complete promise version, and that this hardness persists across quantum resources (any gates, non-Clifford, superposition) and distance metrics. They directly imply PP-hardness of circuit optimization when minimizing total, non-Clifford, or superposition gate depth or count.
